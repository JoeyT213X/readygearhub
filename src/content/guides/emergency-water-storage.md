Clean drinking water may be hard to get after a hurricane. Power outages can stop pumps and treatment plants, flooding can contaminate wells, and a boil-water notice can last after the winds die down. The most reliable plan is to store water *before* the storm, then have a way to treat more if you need it.

## How much water to store

[Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/water) recommends storing **at least one gallon of water per person per day for several days**, for drinking and sanitation. A normally active person needs about three-quarters of a gallon of fluid a day, but needs vary with age, health, activity, and climate. Ready.gov notes that children, nursing mothers, and sick people may need more, a medical emergency may require more, and in very hot weather water needs can double.

The [American Red Cross](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/survival-kit-supplies.html) uses the same one-gallon-per-person-per-day figure, and recommends a **3-day supply for evacuation** and a **2-week supply for home**.

Work from those official amounts, then add water for pets. For a family of four at home, two weeks at one gallon per person per day is 56 gallons, before pets or hotter weather. That is a lot of weight and space, which is why it helps to mix large containers with some smaller, movable water.

Ready.gov's hurricane guidance also notes that after a storm you may not have access to supplies for days or even weeks, so a three-day kit is a starting point, not a ceiling.

## How to store it

Ready.gov's storage advice is straightforward:

- The simplest option is commercially bottled water, kept in the sealed original container in a cool, dark place.
- If you fill your own containers, use food-grade water storage containers. Clean them with dishwashing soap, then sanitize with a solution of one teaspoon of unscented liquid household chlorine bleach to a quart of water, and fill with chlorinated water as Ready.gov describes.
- Water that has not been commercially bottled should be replaced every six months.

Rigid food-grade jugs such as the [Reliance Jumbo-Tainer 2.0](/gear/reliance-jumbo-tainer-2/) (7 gallons) or the [Reliance Aqua-Tainer](/gear/reliance-aqua-tainer/) work well for home storage if you clean, fill, and rotate them as directed. A bathtub bladder such as the [WaterBOB](/gear/waterbob/) is for filling the tub *when a storm is actually coming*, not for year-round storage. Individually packaged pouches such as [Datrex emergency water](/gear/datrex-emergency-water-dx1000w/) are easier to move in a go-bag, but they do not replace the larger home supply you need for drinking *and* sanitation.

Label containers as drinking water, keep them off concrete if the manufacturer recommends it, and do not store water next to gasoline, pesticides, or other chemicals.

## Filters vs straw filters vs purifiers

Stored water is your first line. A filter or purifier is a backup for when that supply runs low, a boil-water notice is in effect and you cannot boil, or you have to use another freshwater source. Official treatment guidance still starts with **boiling** when you can: Ready.gov says boiling is the safest method, and to bring water to a rolling boil for one full minute, then let it cool.

If you cannot boil, choose a device that matches the risk:

- **Straw and squeeze filters** (for example, the [LifeStraw personal filter](/gear/lifestraw-personal/) or [Sawyer Squeeze](/gear/sawyer-squeeze-water-filter/)) typically target bacteria and protozoa. They are light and useful, but you must read the official page for what they do *not* remove. Many do not claim virus removal.
- **Purifiers** such as the [GRAYL UltraPress](/gear/grayl-ultrapress-16-9oz/) or the gravity-fed [LifeStraw Family](/gear/lifestraw-family/) are designed to reduce viruses as well as bacteria and protozoa, according to their manufacturers. That matters more when the water source is uncertain.
- **Clear-water-only filters** such as the [Katadyn BeFree](/gear/katadyn-befree-0-6l/) are not the right tool for cloudy floodwater. Ready.gov says to let sediment settle or strain dirty water before treatment, and floodwater can contain chemicals and debris that a household filter is not designed to handle. Do not drink floodwater if you can avoid it.

No portable filter replaces the need to follow a local boil-water notice and the latest instructions from your utility or health department. Mississippi households can start with the [local resource directory](/local/mississippi/) for official county and utility links.

## Don't forget food that does not need a fridge

Water and food plans go together. If the power is out, you want meals that need no refrigeration. The Red Cross recommends non-perishable, easy-to-prepare food (3 days for evacuation, 2 weeks at home) plus a manual can opener. A compact kit such as the [ReadyWise 72 Hour Food Kit](/gear/readywise-72-hour-food-kit/) covers a short stay; a longer pail such as the [Augason Farms 30-day supply](/gear/augason-farms-30-day-1-person/) is for a longer home reserve. If a meal requires boiling water, plan a safe way to heat it. Fuel-burning stoves and generators belong outdoors, with the same carbon monoxide rules that apply to any fuel-burning equipment.

Store at least the official minimum, keep it drinkable, and treat anything of uncertain quality before you use it for drinking, cooking, or brushing teeth, as Ready.gov advises.
