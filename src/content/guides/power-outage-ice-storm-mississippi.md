Ice storms don't hit Mississippi every winter, but when one does, it can take out power across a wide area. The National Weather Service explains why: heavy ice can bring down trees and topple utility poles, and it can disrupt power and communications for days while crews repair the damage. Here's what to do, in order, once the lights go out.

## The first 30 minutes

- [ ] **Check whether it's just you.** Look at your breaker panel and see whether your neighbors' lights are out too. If only your house is dark, it may be a tripped breaker.
- [ ] **Report the outage to your power company.** That may be Entergy Mississippi, Mississippi Power, or your local electric power association (co-op). Use their outage app, website, or phone line. Don't assume a neighbor already called. Write the number on paper now in case your phone dies.
- [ ] **Unplug sensitive electronics** such as TVs, computers, and game consoles. Ready.gov notes that power can come back with surges that damage equipment. Leave one lamp switched on so you'll know when power returns.
- [ ] **Switch to flashlights, headlamps, or lanterns.** Skip candles if you can, since an open flame in a dark, crowded house is a fire risk.
- [ ] **Turn on a weather radio.** Cell networks can get overloaded or lose power during big storms. A battery or hand-crank radio keeps you hearing NWS alerts and local news.
- [ ] **Save your phone battery.** Turn on low-power mode, dim the screen, and use a power bank or power station to top it up.

## Stay warm without risking carbon monoxide

Losing power in winter usually means losing heat, and that's when people take dangerous shortcuts. Carbon monoxide (CO) is the big one. The CDC describes it as an odorless, colorless gas that can cause sudden illness and death.

**Never do these, no matter how cold it gets:**

- Don't use a gas oven or stovetop to heat your home. Ready.gov and the CDC both warn against it.
- Don't bring a grill, charcoal, camp stove, or generator inside, and that includes the garage, even with the door open.
- Don't burn charcoal indoors. The CDC is clear that burning charcoal gives off CO.

**Do these instead:**

1. **Pick one room to keep warm.** Choose a small interior room with few windows. Close the doors to other rooms, hang blankets over doorways, and roll towels along door gaps.
2. **Layer up.** Wear a hat, wool socks, and several loose layers. Cover up with sleeping bags or blankets, and use a heat-reflective emergency blanket or bivvy as an extra layer.
3. **If you use a fuel-burning heater**, only use one that the manufacturer rates for indoor use. Follow the manufacturer's ventilation instructions exactly, keep it clear of anything that can burn, and **run a working CO alarm** in the same space. Our [Home Heating Backup](/home-heating-backup/) lane lists indoor-rated options like the [Mr. Heater Portable Buddy](/gear/mr-heater-portable-buddy/), along with the warnings that go with them.
4. **Have a battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarm.** The CDC recommends one near every sleeping area. A plug-in alarm without a battery backup won't work in an outage. See the [Eton Blackout Buddy CO detector](/gear/eton-blackout-buddy-co/) listing.

The CDC lists the most common CO poisoning symptoms as **headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion**, often described as "flu-like." If anyone in the house has these symptoms, or the CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air right away and call 911.

## If you have a generator

The CDC says to run portable generators **outside only, more than 20 feet from any windows, doors, and vents**, and never in the home or garage, even with doors and windows open. Ready.gov adds a few more rules:

- Keep the generator dry. Touching a wet generator can cause electric shock.
- Use heavy-duty extension cords to connect appliances.
- Let it cool before refueling, because fuel spilled on a hot engine can ignite.
- Follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Keep a battery-powered CO alarm running inside while the generator runs.

## Keep food and water safe

Ready.gov's food guidance during an outage is simple:

- **Keep the fridge and freezer closed.** A closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours.
- **Use coolers with ice** if the outage runs longer, and use a thermometer to check the temperature.
- **When in doubt, throw it out.** Toss food that has been at 40°F or higher for two hours or more, or that has an odd smell, color, or texture.

If you're on a **well**, the pump won't run without power. Use the water you stored before the storm, and keep a few jugs or a filled bathtub for flushing. If your water system issues a **boil-water notice**, follow it exactly.

## Stay away from downed lines and falling limbs

Ice-heavy trees keep dropping limbs for hours after the freezing rain stops. Stay out from under big trees when you can. **Treat every downed power line as live.** Stay well away from it, keep kids and pets back, and report it to your power company or 911. Never drive over a downed line.

## Stay off the roads if you can

Ready.gov's advice is to stay off roads during winter storms if at all possible. The NWS warns that bridges and overpasses freeze before other surfaces, and that black ice is often hard to see, especially early in the morning. ## Check on people, and know when to leave

Older adults, people with medical needs, and families with babies are at higher risk in a cold house. Check on neighbors by phone or in person if it's safe.

Ready.gov lists these signs of **hypothermia**: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, or drowsiness. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency. Get medical help.

It's time to go somewhere warmer if:

- You can't keep one room reasonably warm.
- Anyone shows signs of hypothermia.
- Someone depends on powered medical equipment and you're running out of backup power.

Ready.gov suggests checking with local officials about heating locations. In Mississippi, watch for announcements from your county emergency management office and MEMA, and listen to local news on your radio.
