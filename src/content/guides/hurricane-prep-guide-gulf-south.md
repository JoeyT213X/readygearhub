Hurricane season on the Gulf Coast is a regular part of life from the Mississippi Coast through coastal Alabama and Louisiana, and it is not only a beach problem. A storm that comes ashore near Biloxi, Mobile, or New Orleans can also flood roads, drop trees, and cut power far inland — in places like Hattiesburg, Jackson, Birmingham, Montgomery, Baton Rouge, and Lafayette. This guide pulls the prep into one place: how to read the alerts, when to leave, how to get the house and the people in it ready, and what to check once the wind dies down.

It's long on purpose. Use the contents below to jump to what you need right now. Every safety number on this page comes from the National Hurricane Center (NHC), the National Weather Service (NWS), Ready.gov (FEMA's preparedness site), the CDC, FoodSafety.gov, or CISA, with a link so you can check it yourself.

> **If a storm is already on top of you:** go straight to [During the storm](#during-the-storm). If local officials tell you to evacuate, leave immediately. Do not walk, swim, or drive through flood waters: Ready.gov notes that just six inches of fast-moving water can knock you down, and one foot of moving water can sweep a vehicle away. Never run a generator, grill, camp stove, or charcoal indoors or in a garage. Call 911 if anyone shows signs of carbon monoxide poisoning.

## Why hurricanes hit the Gulf South differently

The [Atlantic hurricane season](https://www.ready.gov/hurricanes) runs from June 1 through November 30. The [NWS notes](https://www.weather.gov/safety/hurricane) that, on average, 14 tropical storms form over the Atlantic Ocean, Caribbean Sea, or Gulf of America during that season, and 7 of those become hurricanes. Over a typical two-year period, the U.S. coastline is struck by an average of 3 hurricanes, 1 of which is classified as a major hurricane (winds of 111 mph or greater). Those are long-run averages, not a forecast for any one year.

Ready.gov is clear that [hurricanes are not just a coastal problem](https://www.ready.gov/hurricanes). Rain, wind, water, and even tornadoes can happen far inland from where a storm makes landfall. That matters in Mississippi, Alabama, and Louisiana, where a lot of people live well away from the beach but still under the same storm.

The main hazards, from the [NWS](https://www.weather.gov/safety/hurricane) and the [NHC](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/prepare/hazards.php), are:

- **Storm surge.** This is an abnormal rise of water generated by a storm's winds. Ready.gov and the NWS both note that, historically, storm surge is the leading cause of hurricane-related deaths in the United States. The NHC says surge can reach heights well over 20 feet, can span hundreds of miles of coastline, and can travel several miles inland. The NWS adds that surge travels inland especially along bays, rivers, and estuaries — the kind of shoreline the Gulf South has in abundance. In estuaries and bayous, the NHC notes, saltwater intrusion also endangers public health and the environment. Storm tide is surge plus the astronomical tide.
- **Inland flooding from heavy rain.** The NWS calls this the second leading cause of fatalities from landfalling tropical cyclones, and says torrential rains can cause flooding hundreds of miles inland that can persist for several days after a storm has dissipated. The NHC puts it simply: flooding is the major threat from tropical cyclones for people living inland. Those storms often produce widespread rains in excess of 6 inches. Rainfall is not directly tied to the storm's category: slower-moving and larger storms produce more rain.
- **Destructive winds.** Hurricane-force winds are 74 mph or more. The NHC says they can destroy buildings and mobile homes, and that signs, roofing material, siding, and other items left outside can become flying missiles. Winds can stay above hurricane strength well inland.
- **Tornadoes.** These often occur in rain bands well away from the center of the storm, though the NHC notes they can also form near the eyewall.
- **High surf and rip currents.** Dangerous waves and rip currents can affect the coast even when a storm is more than 1,000 miles offshore, according to the NWS.

The [Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/aboutsshws.php) is a 1 to 5 rating based **only** on a hurricane's maximum sustained wind speed. It does not take into account storm surge, rainfall flooding, or tornadoes. The NHC is explicit: hurricanes of all categories can produce deadly surge, rain-induced floods, and tornadoes, and those hazards can mean you need to leave even when the wind category sounds "only" like a 1 or a 2. Category 3 and higher are called major hurricanes (111 mph or greater).

| Category | Sustained winds | How the NHC describes potential wind damage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 74–95 mph | Some damage to roofs, siding, and gutters; power outages that could last a few to several days |
| 2 | 96–110 mph | Major roof and siding damage; near-total power loss that could last several days to weeks |
| 3 (major) | 111–129 mph | Devastating damage; electricity and water may be unavailable for several days to weeks |
| 4 (major) | 130–156 mph | Catastrophic damage; outages can last weeks to possibly months |
| 5 (major) | 157 mph or higher | Catastrophic damage; most of the area may be uninhabitable for weeks or months |

Use that table to understand wind risk. Do not use it as a stand-in for your surge or flood risk. Check your official evacuation zone, and follow local officials.

A few other things make Gulf South storms harder to ride out than a short summer squall:

- **Heat after the power goes out.** [Ready.gov's water guidance](https://www.ready.gov/water) notes that in very hot temperatures, water needs can double. A long outage in August or September is a different problem than a winter ice storm. For winter-specific heat and pipe advice, see our [Winter Storm & Power Outage Prep Guide for the South](/guides/winter-storm-power-outage-prep-guide-south/).
- **Trees, wet ground, and long outages.** Once the soil is soaked, wind brings down limbs and lines. Plan on being able to get by at home for several days, and longer if you live somewhere rural.
- **Coast and inland are different jobs.** On the coast the first question is often "do I leave because of surge?" Inland the first questions are often flooding of roads and creeks, tornadoes in the rain bands, and how long the power and water will be out.

## Know the alerts and where to watch them

The National Hurricane Center is the official source for Atlantic tropical cyclone forecasts. The [NWS explains](https://www.weather.gov/safety/hurricane-ww) that once a tropical cyclone or subtropical storm has formed, the NHC issues advisory products at least every 6 hours (5 a.m., 11 a.m., 5 p.m., and 11 p.m. EDT). When coastal tropical storm or hurricane watches or warnings are in effect, public advisories come out every 3 hours. You can follow them at [hurricanes.gov](https://www.hurricanes.gov/), on [weather.gov](https://www.weather.gov/) for your local forecast office, on TV and radio, on your phone, and on a NOAA Weather Radio.

A **watch** means conditions are possible. A **warning** means they are expected. Because it may not be safe to finish preparations once winds reach tropical storm force, the NHC times hurricane watches and warnings from the arrival of *tropical-storm-force* winds, not from the arrival of hurricane-force winds.

| Alert | What it means | Timing, per the NWS and NHC |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical Storm Watch | Sustained winds of 39 to 73 mph are possible | Within 48 hours |
| Tropical Storm Warning | Sustained winds of 39 to 73 mph are expected | Within 36 hours |
| Hurricane Watch | Sustained winds of 74 mph or greater are possible | Issued 48 hours before anticipated tropical-storm-force winds |
| Hurricane Warning | Sustained winds of 74 mph or greater are expected | Issued 36 hours before anticipated tropical-storm-force winds. All preparations should be complete. Evacuate immediately if ordered. |
| Storm Surge Watch | Life-threatening inundation from water moving inland from the shoreline is possible | Generally within 48 hours |
| Storm Surge Warning | Life-threatening inundation is a danger | Generally within 36 hours. Check for evacuation orders. |
| Extreme Wind Warning | Extreme sustained winds of a major hurricane (115 mph or greater), usually with the eyewall, are expected | Within an hour. Take immediate shelter in the interior of a well-built structure. |

Watches and warnings can also be issued for a "potential tropical cyclone" or a "post-tropical cyclone" when the forecast conditions on land warrant an alert, even if the storm does not (or no longer) meets every technical definition of a tropical cyclone.

**About the forecast cone.** The NWS says the track forecast cone is drawn so that the center of the storm will remain within the cone about 60 to 70 percent of the time. The effects of a tropical cyclone can span hundreds of miles. Areas well outside the cone often experience hazards such as tornadoes or inland flooding from heavy rain. Do not look at a skinny cone over the water and assume you are safe because your town is beside it, not under it.

On this site, you can follow official NWS alerts on [Live Weather Alerts](/alerts/) and in [Storm Tracker](/storm-tracker/), which can cache alerts for a saved county if your data connection drops. A NOAA Weather Radio such as the [Midland ER310](/gear/midland-er310-crank-radio/) still works when the internet and cell network are down. Ready.gov also points people to community alert systems, the Emergency Alert System, Wireless Emergency Alerts (no sign-up required), and the [FEMA app](https://www.ready.gov/hurricanes) for National Weather Service alerts.

## Before the storm: a countdown

### Now, before hurricane season (one-time setup)

Do this in the spring, or any quiet week, so you are not shopping in a crowded store under a watch.

- [ ] **Learn your official evacuation zone and routes** from your state or parish/county emergency management office. Ready.gov's [hurricane](https://www.ready.gov/hurricanes) and [evacuation](https://www.ready.gov/evacuation) pages both stress this. Practice the plan with everyone in the household, including pets.
- [ ] **Make a household plan.** Ready.gov asks that everyone know the plan, and that you identify whether anyone will need extra help, including a person with a disability. Pick an out-of-area contact and a meeting place. See [medical and power-dependent needs](#medical-and-power-dependent-needs) and [pets](#pets).
- [ ] **Check insurance, including flood coverage.** [FEMA](https://www.fema.gov/faq/homeowners-insurance-and-flooding) says flood damage is not typically covered by a homeowners insurance policy. [Flood insurance](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) is a separate policy, available through the National Flood Insurance Program in participating communities, and it can cover a building, the contents, or both. Ask your agent what you have, and what you don't.
- [ ] **Copy important documents** and store them the way Ready.gov describes. Details are in [Protect your accounts and documents](#protect-your-accounts-and-documents).
- [ ] **Install and test carbon monoxide alarms** that have battery backup, and keep extra batteries. See [Power, generators, and carbon monoxide](#power-generators-and-carbon-monoxide).
- [ ] **Build a home kit and a go-bag.** Use the free [Emergency Kit Builder](/tools/kit-builder/), which follows Ready.gov's kit list, and read [Family First Aid Kit for Storms](/guides/storm-first-aid-kit/). A small grab-and-go option lives on the [ReadyWise 5-Day Survival Backpack](/gear/readywise-5-day-survival-backpack/) listing; whatever bag you use, pack what your household actually needs.
- [ ] **Walk the house.** Ready.gov's hurricane guidance is to declutter drains and gutters, bring in outside furniture, and consider hurricane shutters. Fix easy water-entry problems now, not in a 36-hour warning.
- [ ] **Know how you will get alerts** if the power and the cell network are both struggling: a weather radio, a battery or power station, and a planned check of [Live Weather Alerts](/alerts/).

### When a Tropical Storm or Hurricane Watch is issued

A hurricane watch means hurricane-force winds are possible, and it is issued 48 hours before anticipated tropical-storm-force winds. Use that window. Do not wait for the warning if you already know you will leave.

- [ ] **Decide stay or go**, based on your official zone and the latest local instructions, not on the storm's category alone. If you will evacuate, leaving during the watch is often easier than leaving during the warning. See [evacuation](#know-your-evacuation-zone-and-leave-plan).
- [ ] **Fill your water storage** and, if you are staying, consider filling a bathtub bladder such as the [waterBOB](/gear/waterbob/). Use the [Water Storage Calculator](/tools/water-calculator/) and the [water](#water-and-food-safety) section below.
- [ ] **Get cash, fuel, and prescriptions.** Ready.gov's evacuation page says to keep a full tank of gas if an evacuation seems likely, and to keep at least a half tank at all times, because stations may be closed or unable to pump during an outage. Plan on one car per family to reduce congestion.
- [ ] **Charge phones, power banks, and a power station.** Ready.gov says to keep your cell phone charged when a hurricane is in the forecast and to have backup charging devices.
- [ ] **Bring in or tie down anything that can fly:** furniture, grills, trash cans, decorations, and loose tools. The NHC warns that items left outside can become missiles.
- [ ] **Check on neighbors** who may need a ride or an extra hand, which Ready.gov also recommends.
- [ ] **Freeze water jugs and leftovers** to help the freezer ride out an outage. See [food](#water-and-food-safety).

### When a Warning is issued (the last 36 hours)

The NWS is blunt: all preparations should be complete, and you should evacuate immediately if ordered. Tropical-storm-force winds may arrive before the hurricane itself, and outdoor work gets dangerous once they do.

- [ ] **Leave if you are in a mandatory evacuation zone** or if local officials tell you to go.
- [ ] **If you are staying, finish the last indoor steps only:** fill remaining water containers, set out flashlights and a [Black Diamond Spot 400](/gear/black-diamond-spot-400/) or a lantern such as the [Goal Zero Lighthouse 600](/gear/goal-zero-lighthouse-600/), move the kit to one room, and turn on the weather radio.
- [ ] **Park the car on high ground** if you can do it safely, and out from under large limbs.
- [ ] **Do not go back outside to "finish one more thing"** once the wind is up.

## Know your evacuation zone and leave plan

If you live in an evacuation zone, [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/hurricanes) says you may have to leave quickly, and that you should learn your routes, practice with your household and pets, and identify where you will stay. Always follow local emergency managers. [Ready.gov's evacuation page](https://www.ready.gov/evacuation) adds the practical details:

- Know several places you could go — a friend's home in another town, a motel, or a pre-designated shelter — and choose destinations in different directions so you have options.
- Most public shelters allow only service animals, so plan now where pets will stay.
- If you have a car, keep the tank close to full when an evacuation seems likely, and keep a small emergency kit in the car. If you do not have a car, plan how you will leave, including with family, friends, or local emergency management.
- Leave early enough to avoid being trapped by severe weather. Take your kit. Follow recommended routes; do not take shortcuts, which may be blocked.
- Be alert for washed-out roads, bridges, and downed power lines. Do not drive into flooded areas.
- If time allows, tell your out-of-area contact where you are going, secure the house by closing and locking doors and windows, and leave a note with when you left and where you are going.
- Unplug radios, televisions, and small appliances. Leave freezers and refrigerators plugged in unless there is a risk of flooding. If you are instructed to do so, shut off water, gas, and electricity before you go.
- Wear sturdy shoes and clothing that offers some protection.
- Check with neighbors who may need a ride.
- Afterward, check with local officials both where you are staying and back home before you return. Avoid downed lines; they may be live.

Put the household plan, contacts, medicines, and meeting places in one folder you can grab. The paid [Family Emergency Binder](/shop/family-emergency-binder/) is built for that, or you can use a simple envelope and our [Emergency Kit Builder](/tools/kit-builder/). For a stay-at-home outage, the [Apartment Outage Kit](/kits/apartment-outage-kit/) is a useful starting list even if you do not live in an apartment.

## Get the house ready

You cannot storm-proof a house in an afternoon, and this is not a contractor's manual. The goal is to cut the easy risks and to have what you need if you stay.

**Outside**

- Clear gutters and downspouts so heavy rain has somewhere to go. Ready.gov lists decluttering drains and gutters as a basic step.
- Bring in outdoor furniture, toys, flags, and loose tools, or stow them in a closed building. The NWS and NHC both warn that objects left outside can become flying debris.
- If you have hurricane shutters, install them when local officials or the manufacturer say to, and follow the manufacturer's instructions. Ready.gov says to consider shutters; it does not say they are required, and they are not a substitute for leaving a surge zone.
- Move the car and grill away from the house if doing so does not put you in a worse place for flooding or falling limbs.
- Do not stand on a roof or run a chainsaw once a watch has turned into dangerous weather. That work belongs to a calm day, or to a licensed pro after the storm.

**Inside**

- Move the kit, water, flashlights, radio, medications, and important papers to an interior room on the lowest floor that is still safe from flooding. For high winds, Ready.gov says to take refuge in a designated storm shelter or an interior room. For flooding, go to the highest level of the building if you are trapped — and do not climb into a closed attic, where you may be trapped by rising water.
- Fill safe water containers and, if you are staying, the tub. The [waterBOB](/gear/waterbob/) is for filling when a storm is actually coming, not for year-round storage. Rigid jugs such as the [Reliance Jumbo-Tainer 2.0](/gear/reliance-jumbo-tainer-2/) work better for the water you store ahead of time. More on this is in [How Much Water to Store for a Hurricane](/guides/emergency-water-storage/).
- Get flashlights and lanterns ready. The CDC's [after-a-hurricane guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/hurricanes/safety/how-to-safely-stay-safe-after-a-hurricane-or-other-tropical-storm.html) says to use flashlights instead of candles if the power is out. If you must use a candle, keep it away from anything that can catch fire and stay near it.
- Set out a first aid kit such as the [Adventure Medical Kits Adventure First Aid, Family](/gear/amk-adventure-first-aid-family/) and any daily medications. See [Family First Aid Kit for Storms](/guides/storm-first-aid-kit/).
- If you live in an apartment or a small space, the [Apartment Outage Kit](/kits/apartment-outage-kit/) is scaled for that. Battery power is usually a better fit than a gasoline generator when you have no private yard. See [power](#power-generators-and-carbon-monoxide).

## Water and food safety

### Water

[Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/kit) recommends storing **at least one gallon of water per person per day for several days**, for drinking and sanitation. The more detailed [water page](https://www.ready.gov/water) adds that a normally active person needs about three-quarters of a gallon of fluid a day, that children, nursing mothers, and sick people may need more, and that **in very hot temperatures, water needs can double**. That last point matters in the Gulf South in mid-season.

[FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency) says to store at least 1 gallon of water per day for each person and each pet, at least a 3-day supply, and to consider storing more for hot climates, for pregnant women, and for people who are sick. Store water where it will be as safe as possible from flooding, and replace water that has not been commercially bottled every six months.

Use the [Water Storage Calculator](/tools/water-calculator/) to size a household supply, and read [How Much Water to Store for a Hurricane](/guides/emergency-water-storage/) for storage containers, bathtub bladders, and when a filter is a backup rather than a substitute. Ready.gov says boiling is the safest treatment method: bring water to a rolling boil for one full minute, then let it cool. If you cannot boil, follow the chlorination steps on that same Ready.gov water page, and listen to local officials about boil-water notices. Do not use water you have been told is contaminated — or that you suspect is — to make baby formula, make ice, brush your teeth, wash food, wash your hands, or wash dishes, the CDC says. Bottled, boiled, or treated water is the safe option until officials say otherwise.

If you have a private well and the area has flooded, the CDC's [reentering a flooded home](https://www.cdc.gov/floods/safety/reentering-your-flooded-home-safety.html/) guidance is to get the water tested before you use it.

### Food: before the power goes out

FoodSafety.gov recommends at least a **3-day supply** of food that has a long storage life and needs little or no cooking, water, or refrigeration. Ready.gov's kit list likewise calls for a several-day supply of non-perishable food. Ready.gov's hurricane page notes you may not have access to supplies for days or even weeks after a storm, so a three-day kit is a starting point, not a ceiling. Skip very salty or spicy foods if drinking water may be short, FoodSafety.gov says, because those foods increase the need for water.

Before an outage:

- Make sure the refrigerator is at or below 40°F and the freezer is at or below 0°F, and put an appliance thermometer in each, so you can tell later whether food stayed safe.
- Freeze leftovers, milk, and meat you will not need immediately, group food in the freezer so it stays cold longer, and freeze jugs of water. The melting ice is also drinking water.
- Have coolers ready if the power may be out more than 4 hours, and know where you could get dry ice or block ice. FoodSafety.gov notes that 50 pounds of dry ice should keep an 18-cubic-foot, fully stocked freezer cold for two days.

Shelf-stable kits such as the [ReadyWise 5-Day Survival Backpack](/gear/readywise-5-day-survival-backpack/) are one option for a go-bag; a larger home supply can live in regular canned and dry food you already eat, rotated before it expires.

### Food: during and after an outage or a flood

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed. FoodSafety.gov's times, if the door stays shut:

- A refrigerator keeps food cold for about **4 hours**.
- A full freezer holds its temperature for about **48 hours** (about **24 hours** if it is half full).

Refrigerated food should be safe if the power was out no more than 4 hours and the door stayed shut. Discard perishable food (meat, poultry, fish, eggs, leftovers) that has been above 40°F for 2 hours or more, or for **1 hour if the air temperature is above 90°F**. That 90°F rule shows up often after a Gulf Coast outage. You can refreeze or cook freezer food that still contains ice crystals or is 40°F or below. When in doubt, throw it out. The [Outage Timer](/tools/outage-timer/) and the [Cold Hold](/apps/cold-hold/) app can help you keep track.

After a flood, FoodSafety.gov and the CDC both say to throw away food that may have come in contact with flood or storm water, including food in cardboard, in most consumer jars and bottles with screw caps or snap lids, and home-canned food, which cannot be disinfected. Commercially canned food in sealed metal cans or pouches may be salvable if you follow FoodSafety.gov's wash-and-sanitize steps; skip it if a can is bulging, opened, damaged, or smells wrong.

## Power, generators, and carbon monoxide

A hurricane outage can last days to weeks. The goal is light, communications, medical devices, and a way to keep phones charged — not to run the whole house if you cannot do it safely.

Carbon monoxide is a gas you cannot see or smell, and the CDC says it can cause sudden illness and death if it builds up in a home. Fuel-burning equipment that can produce it includes generators, pressure washers, charcoal grills, and camp stoves.

### Generator and fuel-burning equipment rules

Follow the manufacturer's ventilation instructions, and run a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarm whenever anything that burns fuel is in use. If a CO alarm sounds, leave the house right away and call 911.

| Rule | Source |
|---|---|
| Never use a portable gasoline- or coal-burning generator, or a camp stove, inside the home, basement, or garage. Keep it outside and at least **20 feet** from any window, door, or vent. | [CDC, after a hurricane](https://www.cdc.gov/hurricanes/safety/how-to-safely-stay-safe-after-a-hurricane-or-other-tropical-storm.html) |
| Use a battery-operated or battery-backup CO detector any time you use a generator or anything else that burns fuel. | CDC |
| Use generators and fuel only outdoors, at least **20 feet** from windows, doors, and attached garages. | [Ready.gov: Power Outages](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages) |
| Never run a generator inside a home or garage, or connect it to your home's electrical system, unless a licensed electrician has installed the proper transfer equipment. | [Ready.gov: Evacuation](https://www.ready.gov/evacuation), CDC |
| Keep the generator dry. Touching a wet generator or devices connected to one can cause electrical shock. | Ready.gov: Power Outages |
| Connect appliances with heavy-duty, outdoor-rated extension cords. | Ready.gov, CDC |
| Let it cool before refueling. Fuel spilled on hot engine parts can ignite. | Ready.gov |
| Don't store gasoline indoors where fumes could ignite. | CDC, Ready.gov |

**Never plug a generator into a wall outlet.** Feeding power back through house wiring can hurt you and utility crews working on the lines. If you want a generator to power household circuits, have a licensed electrician install a transfer switch. The CDC also notes that it is against the law and a violation of electrical codes to connect a generator to a home's circuits without the approved automatic-interrupt devices.

After a hurricane, this matters even more than usual: people run generators and gas pressure washers during wet cleanup, sometimes on a porch or in an open garage. The CDC's [flooded-home guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/floods/safety/reentering-your-flooded-home-safety.html/) says not to operate a gasoline engine in a home, basement, garage, carport, porch, or other enclosed or partially enclosed structure, or less than 20 feet from a door, window, or vent, even if windows and doors are open.

A working CO alarm such as the [Kidde KN-COB-LP2](/gear/kidde-kn-cob-lp2/), or a unit that also works as a nightlight such as the [Eton Blackout Buddy CO](/gear/eton-blackout-buddy-co/), belongs inside the house before you ever start a generator. Test it, and keep batteries on hand.

### Battery power stations

Battery power stations make no exhaust, so they are the safer choice indoors and in apartments, as long as you follow the manufacturer's instructions and do not try to backfeed the house wiring. ## Medical and power-dependent needs

If someone in the house uses electricity for medical equipment, or needs refrigeration for medicine, make a plan before the first watch. [Ready.gov's guidance for people with disabilities](https://www.ready.gov/disability) is the official starting point: identify extra help you may need, and include that in the household plan.

- Pack a several-day supply of prescription medicine in the go-bag, plus glasses, hearing-aid batteries, and any other daily supplies. Ready.gov notes that after a hurricane you may not have access to these supplies for days or even weeks, and that about half of all Americans take a prescription medicine every day.
- If a device must have power, talk now with the equipment supplier and with local emergency management about what to do if the grid is down — including whether the safer plan is to leave early for a place with reliable power. Do not rely on a gasoline generator indoors to keep medical equipment running.
- Keep paper copies of prescriptions and a list of doctors and pharmacies, in the waterproof documents pouch.
- For aging relatives, the [Elderly Parent Kit](/kits/elderly-parent-kit/) and [Winter Emergency Kit for Elderly Parents](/guides/winter-emergency-kit-for-elderly-parents/) are written around outages and medical needs; most of the kit advice still applies in hurricane season, minus the ice-and-pipe parts.

This is general preparedness information, not medical advice. In an emergency, call 911.

## Pets

[Ready.gov's pet guidance](https://www.ready.gov/pets) and its evacuation page both say to take pets with you, and to plan now how you will care for them. Most public shelters allow only service animals, so identify a pet-friendly hotel, a kennel, or a friend outside the evacuation zone before you need one.

- Pack a go-bag for each animal: food, water (FoodSafety.gov counts one gallon per pet per day in the household water total), medicines, a leash or carrier, waste bags, and copies of vaccination records.
- Make sure your pet has a current ID tag, and keep a recent photo on your phone.
- The [Collar Card](/apps/collar-card/) app stores a pet emergency profile you can show if you get separated.

Livestock and horses need their own plan with your agricultural extension office or local emergency management; do not wait until a warning to decide where they will go.

## Protect your accounts and documents

If you have to leave home, or if the house takes on water, you will want copies of IDs, insurance policies, and other key papers you can still reach. [Ready.gov's emergency kit list](https://www.ready.gov/kit) includes copies of insurance policies, identification, and bank account records, saved electronically or in a waterproof, portable container. [Ready.gov's financial preparedness page](https://www.ready.gov/financial-preparedness) also suggests storing important documents in a safety deposit box, on an external drive, or in the cloud so you can get to them during a disaster.

A password manager can keep logins in one place, and many also let you store secure copies of documents — IDs, insurance cards, and policies — so you can open them from a phone if you have to leave the house. [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords) recommends a password manager for creating and storing long, unique passwords, and notes that some are free (including the one built into your browser) and some cost money. <a href="https://www.dpbolvw.net/click-101929817-13868703" target="_blank" rel="sponsored nofollow noopener" data-program="nordpass">NordPass</a> is one paid subscription option. Use a strong, unique passphrase for the manager itself, and turn on multi-factor authentication if it's offered.

Keep paper copies too, in a waterproof container you can grab quickly, in case phones and internet are down.

**On shelter or public Wi-Fi.** If you end up at a shelter, hotel, or other public network, treat that connection as untrusted. [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/good-security-habits) says public networks are not very secure, which makes it easier for others to intercept your data, and that if you connect to an open network you can use a virtual private network (VPN) to help keep your traffic private. <a href="https://www.anrdoezrs.net/click-101929817-12814518" target="_blank" rel="sponsored nofollow noopener" data-program="nordvpn">NordVPN</a> is a paid subscription VPN. A VPN is optional, not a requirement, and it doesn't replace careful habits: confirm the network name with staff when you can, avoid entering sensitive information if you have a better option (such as your phone's cellular data), and be cautious with unexpected login pages.

## During the storm

- **Stay informed.** Pay attention to official alerts. A weather radio and [Storm Tracker](/storm-tracker/) both help if the TV and the cell network drop out.
- **Leave if you are told to leave.** Ready.gov: if you live in a mandatory evacuation zone and local officials tell you to evacuate, do so immediately.
- **If you are riding it out in a sturdy building,** take refuge in a designated storm shelter or an interior room, away from windows, for high winds. If you are trapped by flooding, go to the highest level of the building. Do not climb into a closed attic. You may become trapped by rising water.
- **Do not walk, swim, or drive through flood waters.** Ready.gov: just six inches of fast-moving water can knock you down, and one foot of moving water can sweep your vehicle away. "Turn Around. Don't Drown."
- **Stay inside until officials say it is safe.** Wind, tornadoes in the rain bands, and downed lines remain a threat after the worst of the rain has passed.
- **Use flashlights, not candles,** if you can, as the CDC recommends.
- **Save phone battery.** Ready.gov suggests saving voice calls for emergencies after a disaster and using text messages when you can, because phone systems are often down or busy.

The [All Accounted](/apps/all-accounted/) app is a simple household check-in board if people are in different rooms or different places.

If an [Extreme Wind Warning](https://www.weather.gov/safety/hurricane-ww) is issued, extreme sustained winds of 115 mph or greater are expected to begin within an hour. Take immediate shelter in the interior of a well-built structure.

## After the storm

The storm ending is not the same thing as it being safe. Follow local officials for information and special instructions, and use official sources — your county or parish emergency management office, your utility, and NWS — rather than rumors.

**Getting around and going back inside**

- Stay out of floodwater. The CDC says it can contain germs, chemicals, human and livestock waste, animals, and downed power lines. Ready.gov adds that underground or downed lines can electrically charge the water, and that you should not wade in it. Wash with soap and safe water if you have been in floodwater; if you don't have those, the CDC says to use alcohol-based wipes or sanitizer.
- Do not drive in flooded areas. Cars will not protect you; they can stall or be swept away.
- Stay away from fallen power lines and report them to the utility. Do not touch electrical equipment if it is wet or if you are standing in water. If it is safe, Ready.gov says to turn off electricity at the main breaker or fuse box to prevent shock.
- Do not enter a damaged building until local authorities say it is safe, the CDC says. Leave if you hear shifting or unusual noises.
- If you evacuated, check with officials where you are and back home before you travel. Ready.gov warns that returning before debris is cleared is dangerous. Charge devices, fill the gas tank, and bring water and food for the ride.

**Cleanup and carbon monoxide**

- Wear protective clothing. Ready.gov says to use appropriate face coverings or masks if you are cleaning mold or debris, to work with someone else, and that children should not help with disaster cleanup. People with asthma, other lung conditions, or immune suppression should not enter buildings with indoor water leaks or mold growth that can be seen or smelled, even if they are not allergic to mold.
- Generators and gas pressure washers used during cleanup follow the same [20-foot, outdoor-only, CO-alarm rules](#power-generators-and-carbon-monoxide) as they did during the outage.
- The CDC's [reentering a flooded home](https://www.cdc.gov/floods/safety/reentering-your-flooded-home-safety.html/) page covers drying, mold, and having a flooded HVAC system checked before you turn it on.

**Food, water, and health**

- Follow local advice on whether tap water is safe. When in doubt, use bottled, boiled, or treated water, and follow the [food and water](#water-and-food-safety) rules above, including throwing out food that touched floodwater.
- Take care of wounds quickly to help prevent infection, the CDC says. This is not medical advice; get care when you need it.
- Strong emotions after a hurricane are common. The CDC points people to SAMHSA's Disaster Distress Helpline at 1-800-985-5990 (call or text).

**Insurance, repairs, and records**

- Document property damage with photographs, Ready.gov says, and contact your insurance company. Keep receipts for emergency repairs.
- Remember the FEMA distinction: flood damage is not typically covered by a homeowners policy, and flood insurance is a separate policy. Wind damage and flood damage are often handled by different policies, so you may be working with more than one adjuster.
- Restock water, medicine, fuel, and batteries once it is safe to travel, and replace anything you used from the kit. The [Aid Dates](/apps/aid-dates/) app can track expiration dates on first-aid supplies.

**A note on repair costs:** a damaged air conditioner, water heater, or other major system after a long outage or a storm can be an expensive surprise. Some homeowners choose a <a href="https://www.dpbolvw.net/click-101929817-12337172" target="_blank" rel="sponsored nofollow noopener" data-program="choice-home-warranty">home warranty plan from Choice Home Warranty</a> (sponsored), which is a paid monthly or yearly service plan that can cover repair or replacement of some major systems and appliances. It's for homeowners only, it isn't insurance, it is not a substitute for homeowners or flood insurance, and coverage has limits, so check the price and read the terms before you sign up.

If you need federal disaster-assistance information after a declaration, FEMA points survivors to [disasterassistance.gov](https://www.disasterassistance.gov/) and the FEMA Helpline at 1-800-621-3362. That is official aid information, not a product offer.

## Special cases

### Mobile and manufactured homes

The NHC says hurricane-force winds, 74 mph or more, can destroy buildings and mobile homes. If local officials tell you to leave, leave. A manufactured home is not a substitute for a designated storm shelter, and surge or inland flooding can move a home off its supports. Have a leave plan and a place to go before the watch is issued.

### Apartments and close neighbors

If you do not have a private yard, skip gasoline generators. Use flashlights, a weather radio, and a battery power station sized for phones, medical devices, and a fan or CPAP if you need one. Know how your building will communicate, where the stairwells are, and whether anyone on your floor needs a ride out. The [Apartment Outage Kit](/kits/apartment-outage-kit/) is a practical starting list.

### Rural wells, pumps, and long distances

If your water comes from a well, a power outage stops the pump. Store water before the storm, and after flooding have the well tested before you drink from it, following the CDC. Rural households should plan on being on their own longer: stores, pharmacies, and help may be farther away, and roads may be blocked by trees or high water.

### Coastal vs. inland

On the Mississippi, Alabama, and Louisiana coasts, treat official evacuation zones and storm-surge watches or warnings as the first decision. Inland, do not relax just because you are "not on the beach." The NWS and NHC both warn that flooding, tornadoes, and damaging winds can occur well inland, and that flooding can last for days after the storm has moved on. Watch for flooded creeks, low water crossings, and rain-band tornadoes, and plan for a multi-day outage either way.

### Coastal visitors and rip currents

Even when a storm is still far out in the Gulf or the Atlantic, the NWS warns that deadly rip currents and high surf can reach the shore. If you are visiting the coast during hurricane season, follow local beach flags and lifeguard instructions, and do not assume a distant storm is irrelevant.
