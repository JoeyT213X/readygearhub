When a winter storm knocks out the power, most homes lose heat too. Even gas furnaces usually need electricity for the blower and controls. The good news is that you can stay reasonably warm for a long time without any heater at all, if you focus on keeping *people* warm instead of the whole house. Here's how to do it without creating a bigger emergency.

## First, what never to use for heat

This comes first because it's the part that hurts people. Ready.gov's winter guidance names **carbon monoxide poisoning** as one of the main risks of winter storms. The CDC calls CO an odorless, colorless gas that can cause sudden illness and death.

According to the CDC and Ready.gov, **never** use these to heat your home:

- A **gas oven or stovetop.** Ready.gov notes it also raises the risk of fires and burns.
- A **grill, charcoal, or a portable camp stove**, indoors or in a garage.
- A **generator**, inside the house or garage, even with the doors and windows open. The CDC says to run it outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents.
- **Portable flameless chemical heaters** indoors. The CDC lists these alongside camp stoves.

Also, don't run a car in an attached garage to warm up, even with the garage door open. The CDC warns against this specifically.

## Step 1: Make one warm room

Heating one small space is much easier than heating a whole house.

- [ ] **Pick an interior room** with the fewest windows and outside walls. A room on the sunny side of the house is a bonus during the day.
- [ ] **Close it off.** Shut doors to unused rooms. Hang a blanket over open doorways. Roll towels along the bottom of doors.
- [ ] **Cover the windows** with heavy curtains or blankets at night. During the day, open the curtains on sunny windows to let the sun in, then close them at dusk.
- [ ] **Bring everyone together.** People, and pets, give off heat. A family in one room stays warmer than everyone spread out.
- [ ] **Build a "warm zone."** Pitch a small tent indoors or make a blanket fort over a bed. Trapping air in a small space makes a real difference, especially for kids.

## Step 2: Dress like you're going outside

- **Layers beat one heavy coat.** Start with a base layer, add a warm middle layer like fleece or wool, and put a windproof layer on top if there's a draft.
- **Wool or synthetics over cotton.** Cotton holds moisture and stops insulating once it's damp.
- **Hat, socks, and gloves, indoors.** Keep your feet dry. Change into dry socks if they get damp. Thick wool socks like [Darn Tough's full-cushion boot socks](/gear/darn-tough-hiker-boot-full-cushion/) are made for this.
- **Sleep warm.** Use sleeping bags if you have them, and pile blankets *under* you as well as on top, since the floor and mattress pull heat away. A heat-reflective [SOL Emergency Bivvy](/gear/sol-emergency-bivvy/) or [emergency blanket](/gear/sol-emergency-blanket/) adds a layer.

## Step 3: Warm the body, not the air

- **Eat and drink.** Your body makes heat by digesting food. Warm drinks help if you can heat water safely. Ready.gov's power outage guidance says camp stoves belong outdoors.
- **Move a little.** Light activity like walking around the room or stretching warms you up. Don't overdo it to the point of sweating.
- **Use hand warmers.** Single-use packs or a rechargeable one like the [Zippo HeatBank 9s](/gear/zippo-heatbank-9s/) are handy at night or for kids.
- **Hot water bottles.** If you have a safe way to heat water, a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel at the foot of the bed works well.

## Step 4: Use backup heat only the safe way

If you have a fuel-burning heater (propane, kerosene, or similar), ReadyGearHub's rule is simple: **only use a heater the manufacturer rates for indoor use, follow the manufacturer's ventilation instructions exactly, and always run a working carbon monoxide alarm.**

- Keep the heater well clear of bedding, curtains, and anything else that can burn.
- Read the manual's guidance on fuel storage, refueling, and whether the heater can be left unattended or used while you sleep, and follow it.
- Our [Home Heating Backup](/home-heating-backup/) lane lists indoor-rated options like the [Mr. Heater Portable Buddy](/gear/mr-heater-portable-buddy/) and [Buddy FLEX](/gear/mr-heater-buddy-flex/). Check Mr. Heater's official page for each model's ratings and instructions before you buy.

**CO alarm:** The CDC recommends battery-operated or battery-backup CO detectors near every sleeping area, with replacement on the manufacturer's schedule or every 5 years. A plug-in alarm without a battery won't help during an outage. See the [Eton Blackout Buddy CO detector](/gear/eton-blackout-buddy-co/).

**Know the symptoms.** The CDC lists headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion. People who are asleep can die from CO before they notice anything. If the alarm sounds or anyone feels sick, get everyone into fresh air and call 911.

**Fireplaces:** a wood fireplace or stove can help if it's in good working order. The CDC recommends having your chimney checked or cleaned every year, because a blocked chimney can let CO build up inside.

## What about a portable power station?

A battery power station is safe to use indoors because it makes no fumes. It's great for lights, phones, a router, and medical devices. It's **not** a good way to run a space heater. Space heaters often draw 750–1,500 watts, which drains most portable batteries fast. A low-wattage electric blanket or heated pad is a better match. Check the watts on its label and compare that with the station's capacity. ## Watch for cold-weather illness

Ready.gov lists these warning signs:

- **Hypothermia:** shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, or drowsiness. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency. Get to a warm place, warm the center of the body first (chest, neck, head, and groin), and keep the person dry and wrapped in blankets.
- **Frostbite:** numbness, white or grayish-yellow skin, or firm or waxy skin. Get to a warm room and soak in warm water. Don't massage the area or use a heating pad.

Infants, older adults, and people with health conditions get cold faster. Check on them often.

## When to leave

If you can't keep a room warm, someone is showing signs of hypothermia, or someone depends on medical equipment and your backup power is running low, go somewhere with heat. Ready.gov suggests checking with local officials about warming locations. Your weather radio, the [What's happening now](/#live-status) board, [live alerts](/alerts/), and local news will usually carry the announcements. You can also follow occasional official-source notes on [Local & community safety updates](/updates/). ## Common questions

**Can I heat the room with the gas oven if I crack a window?**
No. The CDC says never to heat your house with a gas oven, and never to use a portable gas camp stove or charcoal indoors. Opening a window does not make those methods safe.

**How far away should a generator be?**
The CDC and Ready.gov say to use a generator only outdoors, more than 20 feet (at least 20 feet) from windows, doors, and vents, and not in a garage. See the [carbon monoxide safety](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-during-an-outage/) guide.

**Where do warming centers get announced?**
Ready.gov says to check with local officials. In Mississippi, use your [county emergency-management page](/local/mississippi/) and MEMA.
