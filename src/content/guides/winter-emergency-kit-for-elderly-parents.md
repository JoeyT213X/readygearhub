A winter storm is harder on older adults. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) notes that people over 65 often make less body heat, and it recommends checking on older friends and neighbors and, if you are over 65, checking the indoor temperature often in extreme cold. Ice also raises the chance of a fall. The kit below is for an adult child, caregiver, or neighbor who wants to help a parent get ready *before* the forecast turns.

[Ready.gov's older-adults page](https://www.ready.gov/older-adults) is a good official starting point: assess needs, make a plan, and build a support network, not just a box of supplies.

## Start with a plan, not only a bag

- [ ] **Name a support network.** Ready.gov recommends family, friends, caregivers, neighbors, or people from a place of worship who can help in an emergency. At least one person should have an extra house key, know where supplies are kept, and know how to use any lifesaving equipment or help with medicine.
- [ ] **Set a check-in routine** before the storm: who calls or texts, how often, and what happens if there is no answer (a neighbor knocks, or you request a welfare check). Write the plan on paper, not only in a phone.
- [ ] **Write down** doctors, pharmacy, medical conditions, allergies, insurance or Medicare and Medicaid numbers, and an emergency contact list. Ready.gov suggests copies of those cards. Keep the packet in a labeled folder in the kit.
- [ ] **Ask about medical equipment now.** If a parent uses a CPAP, oxygen concentrator, lift chair, powered wheelchair, or refrigerated medicine, talk to the device supplier or clinician about how long a battery lasts and what to do in an outage. A compact [power station](/gear/jackery-explorer-300-v2/) or a charged [power bank](/gear/anker-prime-power-bank-20k-220w/) can cover phones and some devices; confirm the device's actual wattage and the manufacturer's backup-power guidance rather than guessing.
- [ ] **Ask the utility** whether a medical-need or priority-restoration program exists, and what documentation it requires. Do not count on faster restoration; it is only one layer.
- [ ] If the parent receives routine treatments at a clinic or hospital, Ready.gov suggests asking about that facility's emergency plan and a backup provider.

## What to put in the home kit

Store the kit in one or two easy-to-reach containers, on a waist-high shelf if bending or stairs are hard. [Ready.gov's basic kit list](https://www.ready.gov/kit) is the backbone; add the items an older adult actually uses.

**Health and daily living**

- [ ] Prescription medications, inhalers, and a printed list of names, doses, and schedules. Ask the pharmacist or doctor about having enough on hand if pharmacies close, and about a backup plan for any medicine that must stay cold (an insulated bag and frozen packs, per the clinician's instructions).
- [ ] Glasses, hearing-aid batteries, dentures and solution, mobility aids, and a spare cane or walker if they have one. Plan how assistive devices go along in an evacuation, as Ready.gov advises.
- [ ] Non-prescription basics the parent already uses (pain reliever, antacid, or similar), plus any wound or skin-care supplies they need.
- [ ] Easy-to-eat, several-day supply of non-perishable food that matches their diet, a manual can opener, and utensils they can actually use. [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency) recommends at least a 3-day food supply and foods that need little cooking or refrigeration. Skip very salty or spicy foods if drinking water may be limited.
- [ ] About **1 gallon of water per person per day** for several days, which is the Ready.gov guideline, plus extra if the parent is ill. Include water for a pet or service animal.

**Warmth, light, and alerts**

- [ ] Extra blankets or a [sleeping bag](/gear/teton-sports-celsius-regular-0/), warm hat, gloves, and dry socks. A foam pad under a chair or cot helps if they have to rest in a cold room.
- [ ] [Hand warmers](/gear/hothands-hand-warmers/) used only as the package directs. Kobayashi notes that people with reduced sensation to heat, including some older adults, should be careful with direct skin contact.
- [ ] Flashlights or a headlamp, extra batteries, and a [weather radio](/gear/midland-wr400/) so alerts still come through if the TV and phone fail.
- [ ] A charged phone, charging cable, and backup battery, kept within reach of the usual chair or bed.
- [ ] Working [carbon monoxide](/gear/kidde-kn-cob-lp2/) and smoke alarms with fresh batteries, tested before the storm. Never use a generator, grill, camp stove, or charcoal indoors or in a garage; the CDC says to keep generators at least 20 feet from the home.

**Comfort and communications**

- [ ] Cash in small bills, a paper list of phone numbers, and a spare house key for the support person.
- [ ] Incontinence supplies, wipes, and any other personal-care items they would not want to run out of.
- [ ] Pet food, leashes, and vaccination records if a service animal or pet lives there. Ready.gov notes that not all shelters take pets.

## Ice, falls, and going outside

The CDC is direct: walking on ice is extremely dangerous, and many winter injuries are falls on steps, driveways, and porches. For an older parent, the default should be **stay inside** until someone has treated the walkway.

- Have rock salt or another de-icer and a bag of sand at the door *before* the storm, and ask a neighbor or service to clear steps rather than leaving a parent to chip ice.
- If they must step outside, [footwear traction](/gear/kahtoola-microspikes/) and a sturdy cane or walker help, but they do not make ice safe.
- The CDC and Ready.gov both warn against overexertion in the cold, including shoveling, which can strain the heart. If a parent has heart disease or high blood pressure, follow the doctor's advice and arrange help instead.

## During an outage at their house

Keep them in one closed-off room with the supplies, follow the [safe-heating guidance](/guides/heat-one-room-safely-in-an-outage/), and use a battery-powered thermometer to watch indoor temperature. If the house cannot be kept reasonably warm, if a CO alarm sounds, or if the parent becomes confused, drowsy, or unusually quiet, move them to a warming center or to someone with heat. Confusion and drowsiness can be signs of hypothermia, which Ready.gov treats as an emergency when body temperature drops below 95°F.
