When the lights go out at night, the first thing everyone does is reach for a phone. That works for a minute, but it drains the battery you may need for calls and alerts. A few good lights, kept where you can find them in the dark, make an outage calmer and safer.

The American Red Cross puts it simply: [use flashlights, not candles](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/types-of-emergencies/power-outage.html). Ready.gov lists a flashlight and extra batteries in its [basic emergency kit](https://www.ready.gov/kit). This guide helps you choose lights that fit your home and keep them ready.

## Start with a headlamp for every adult

If you buy one light, make it a headlamp. It points wherever you look and leaves both hands free, which matters when you are:

- Carrying a child or a pet
- Pouring water, cooking on a camp stove outdoors, or finding medicine
- Checking a breaker panel or the water heater
- Walking down stairs while holding the railing

Look for a headlamp with a low mode for reading and moving around the house, a red light mode that won't blind the person next to you, and a comfortable strap. The [Wuben H1](/gear/wuben-h1/) is a rechargeable outdoor headlamp listed at 1,200 lumens, and the [Wuben E7](/gear/wuben-e7/) is listed at 1,800. The [Wuben H4](/gear/wuben-h4/) is another rechargeable option. For inside the house, you'll spend most of your time on the lowest settings anyway.

## How bright is bright enough?

Lumens measure how much light comes out. Big numbers sell flashlights, but in a dark house you rarely need the top setting.

A practical way to think about it:

- **Low modes** are what you'll use most in a dark house: moving room to room, reading, getting kids ready for bed. Low also runs the longest.
- **Medium modes** suit chores, like cooking or looking through a closet.
- **High and turbo modes** are for outdoors: checking the yard, a downed limb, or the street. Some lights only hold their highest level for a short time before stepping down to protect the battery and keep the light from getting too hot.

Makers list lumens and runtime for each mode. Compare the runtime at the mode you'll actually use, not the headline number.

## Rechargeable or regular batteries?

Both work. Most households do best with a mix.

**Rechargeable lights** (USB-C is the most convenient) are cheaper to run and easy to top up from a power bank, a car charger or an Energy Reserve. Some double as a small power bank for a phone. The [Wuben E12R](/gear/wuben-e12r/) and [Wuben C2](/gear/wuben-c2/) list a power-bank function on Wuben's pages.

The catch: a rechargeable light is only ready if you charged it. Put them on a charging routine, like the first of every month or whenever a storm shows up in the forecast.

**Lights that take AA or AAA batteries** can sit in a drawer for a long time, and you can swap in fresh cells. If you go this way, keep spare batteries in their package, check the date, and store them somewhere cool and dry.

A good mix: rechargeable headlamps and flashlights for daily use and charging before storms, plus one or two lights that take standard batteries as a backup.

## Area lights: light up a room, not just a spot

A flashlight lights a spot. A lantern lights a room, which is what you want for dinner, a card game, or a bathroom at night. The [Wuben F5](/gear/wuben-f5/) is a rechargeable camping and tent light that works as a room light in an outage.

A simple trick works with any flashlight too: stand it on its tail and point the beam at a white ceiling, or set it behind a water jug or a white plastic bottle. The light spreads out and fills more of the room.

## Small lights you'll actually carry

The light you have on you is the one you'll use. A small everyday-carry light in a pocket, purse or on a keychain helps in a dark parking lot, a stalled elevator or a sudden outage at work.

- The [Wuben G5](/gear/wuben-g5/) is a small keychain light listed at 400 lumens.
- The [Wuben Lightok X0](/gear/wuben-lightok-x0/) and [Wuben C3](/gear/wuben-c3/) are compact pocket lights listed at 1,100 and 1,200 lumens.

## One big light for outside

After a storm, you may need to look at the roof line, a fallen tree or flooding in the street. A larger handheld light with a long beam helps here. The [Wuben A22](/gear/wuben-a22/) is listed at 4,500 lumens. Use high modes outside, and don't shine them in people's eyes or at drivers.

Stay away from downed power lines. Treat every downed line as live, keep your distance, and report it to your utility.

## A room-by-room lighting plan

Lights only help if you can find them in the dark. Give each one a home and tell everyone where it is.

| Where | What to keep | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Each bedroom nightstand | A headlamp | Hands-free light the moment you wake up |
| Kitchen drawer | A flashlight and spare batteries | The first place most people look |
| Living room | A lantern or area light | Lights the whole room for the family |
| Bathroom | A small light | Nighttime trips without a phone |
| Garage or utility area | A bright handheld | Breaker panel, water heater and outdoor checks |
| Car glove box | A small light and spare batteries | Roadside trouble after dark |
| Go-bag | A headlamp | Evacuations and shelters |

## Light for the long evening

Once the lights are sorted, a long outage night goes easier with something to do. A deck of cards or a board game needs no outlet, and a charged Bluetooth speaker can play music or a downloaded podcast without touching your phone's battery much. Browse [off-grid games and gifts](/directory/fun/) and [outage-ready speakers and headphones](/directory/audio/). Keep a crank or battery weather radio for official alerts.

## Keep them ready

- **Charge on a schedule.** Pick a date each month, and charge everything when a storm is in the forecast.
- **Test twice a year.** When you change the clocks is a good reminder. Turn every light on.
- **Store batteries the right way.** Keep spares in their packaging, away from metal objects like keys and coins.
- **Remove batteries from lights you won't use for a long time** if the maker recommends it, so a leaking cell doesn't ruin the light.
- **Teach kids** where their light lives and how to turn it on.

## What not to do

- **Skip candles.** The Red Cross recommends flashlights instead.
- **Never use a fuel lantern, camp stove or grill indoors** for light or heat. These make carbon monoxide. See our [winter power outage checklist](/guides/winter-power-outage-checklist/) for the full safety list.
- **Don't drain your phone** as your main light. Save it for calls, alerts and maps.

## The bottom line

Give every adult a headlamp, put a room light where the family gathers, keep a small light in every bag and car, and charge everything on a schedule. That's most of what a good outage lighting plan needs.
