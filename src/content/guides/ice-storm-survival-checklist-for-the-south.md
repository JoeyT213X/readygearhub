Ice storms in the South are often brief, but they can be unusually disruptive. Freezing rain loads trees, power lines, and roads with ice. Homes built for milder winters may be drafty, and a heat pump or well pump can stop as soon as the electricity goes out. This checklist is written for that kind of event, not a long Northern blizzard.

Pay attention to National Weather Service products: a **Winter Storm Watch** means ice, sleet, or heavy snow is possible, usually 12 to 48 hours out; a **Winter Storm Warning** means hazardous winter weather is imminent or occurring, usually 12 to 24 hours out, according to [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather). A Winter Weather Advisory still deserves caution. Sign up for local alerts and keep a [NOAA weather radio](/gear/midland-wr400/) ready if the cell network fails.

## Before the freezing rain starts

**Water, food, and medicines**

- [ ] Store drinking water now, while the tap still works. [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/kit) recommends about **1 gallon of water per person per day** for several days, for drinking and sanitation. Count pets too. A [Reliance Aqua-Tainer](/gear/reliance-aqua-tainer/) or a [waterBOB](/gear/waterbob/) bathtub liner can hold a larger reserve if a well pump or water plant loses power.
- [ ] Stock several days of food that needs little or no cooking or refrigeration, plus a manual can opener, as [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency) recommends. Put a thermometer in the fridge and freezer so you can check temperatures later.
- [ ] Refill prescriptions and set aside medicines, medical supplies, batteries, and chargers, as [Ready.gov's older-adults guidance](https://www.ready.gov/older-adults) also emphasizes.

**Heat, pipes, and the house**

- [ ] Test [smoke alarms and carbon monoxide alarms](/gear/kidde-kn-cob-lp2/) with battery backup on every level, especially near bedrooms. Ready.gov lists this as basic winter prep.
- [ ] Cut drafts you can reach. Close storm windows, put towels at door gaps, and consider an [indoor window insulator kit](/gear/3m-indoor-window-insulator-kit/) on the leakiest panes.
- [ ] Know where the main water shut-off is. Open cabinet doors under sinks so warm air can reach pipes, and disconnect outdoor hoses.
- [ ] Charge phones, power banks, and any portable energy reserve. Gather flashlights or headlamps and extra batteries. Use battery lanterns rather than candles if you can; the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) notes that candles can lead to house fires.
- [ ] Bring in pets, patio plants you care about, and any loose yard items that ice or wind could turn into debris.

**Car and travel**

- [ ] Fill the gas tank. Pumps need electricity, and you may not want to drive later.
- [ ] Put a winter kit in the car (blanket, water, snacks, flashlight, phone charger, ice scraper) as Ready.gov recommends, plus [footwear traction](/gear/kahtoola-microspikes/) if you have it.
- [ ] Plan to stay home once ice starts. Ready.gov's advice is to stay off roads if at all possible.

## During the ice and the outage

1. **Stay inside if you can.** Walking on ice is extremely dangerous, the CDC notes, and many winter injuries come from falls on sidewalks, steps, driveways, and porches. If you must go out, wear shoes with traction, take small steps, and use handrails.
2. **Stay far from downed or sagging power lines**, and from anything they are touching, including fences and puddles. Report them to the utility. After an ice storm, limbs and lines can still fall as ice melts or the wind shifts.
3. **Do not run a generator, grill, camp stove, or charcoal indoors**, in a garage, or near windows. Ready.gov and the CDC both say generators belong outdoors and **at least 20 feet** from windows, doors, and attached garages. Never heat the house with a gas stovetop or oven. Keep a working CO alarm on. See [carbon monoxide safety during a power outage](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-during-an-outage/).
4. **Protect pipes.** If you still have water pressure, the CDC recommends leaving taps slightly open so they drip, keeping the house as warm as you reasonably can, and leaving sink-cabinet doors open. If pipes freeze, do not thaw them with a torch.
5. **Pick one room to keep warm**, close unused rooms, and cover windows at night. Extra blankets, a [sleeping bag](/gear/teton-sports-celsius-regular-0/), and dry socks matter more than trying to heat the whole house. See our guide on [heating one room safely in an outage](/guides/heat-one-room-safely-in-an-outage/).
6. **Keep the fridge and freezer closed.** FoodSafety.gov says a closed refrigerator keeps food cold about 4 hours, a full closed freezer about 48 hours, and a half-full freezer about 24 hours.
7. **Listen for local alerts**, including boil-water notices some utilities issue after pumps or treatment plants lose power. Follow the notice if one is issued.
8. **Watch for hypothermia**: shivering, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, or drowsiness. Ready.gov says a body temperature below 95°F is an emergency. Check on neighbors, especially older adults.

Skip the heroic outdoor cleanup while ice is still falling or clinging to trees. Ready.gov also warns that overexertion in winter weather raises the risk of a heart attack.

## After the ice melts

- [ ] Wait for official word that roads are passable. Bridges, overpasses, and shaded spots can stay icy after the sun comes out.
- [ ] Look from a safe distance for hanging limbs, damaged service lines, and roof or gutter ice before you walk the yard.
- [ ] When the power returns, check that the furnace or heat pump restarts, then inspect for frozen or burst pipes before opening the water fully.
- [ ] Throw out refrigerated perishables that sat above 40°F for more than 2 hours. When in doubt, throw it out, as FoodSafety.gov advises.
- [ ] Recharge devices, refill water, and restock what you used.

## Common questions

**When should I throw food out after the power returns?**
FoodSafety.gov says to throw out refrigerated perishables that sat above 40°F for more than 2 hours. When in doubt, throw it out. A closed fridge holds safe temperatures only about 4 hours; a full closed freezer about 48 hours.

**How do I find official warming or cooling sites?**
We do not keep a static address list, because sites open and close as officials need them. Use your [county emergency-management page](/local/mississippi/) and [MEMA's shelters list](https://www.msema.org/get-assistance/disaster-resources/shelters-and-safe-rooms).

**Is a NOAA weather radio still useful if I have Wireless Emergency Alerts on my phone?**
Yes. Phone batteries die and cell service can fail in a long outage. Ready.gov lists both Wireless Emergency Alerts and NOAA Weather Radio as official alert channels.
