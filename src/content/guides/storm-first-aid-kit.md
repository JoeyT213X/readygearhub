A family first aid kit will not replace professional care, but it can help you handle minor injuries and support someone until help arrives. This page is **not medical advice**. For training, take a first-aid and CPR course from the [American Red Cross](https://www.redcross.org/) or another qualified provider, and call 911 in an emergency.

## Start with official kit guidance

The Red Cross [survival kit list](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/survival-kit-supplies.html) includes a first aid kit, plus:

- Medications (a 7-day supply) and medical items
- Copies of personal documents, including a medication list and pertinent medical information
- Sanitation and personal hygiene items
- An emergency blanket
- Extra items that are unique to your household, such as glasses, hearing-aid batteries, syringes, or infant supplies

[Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/kit) likewise includes a first aid kit among basic emergency supplies, and Ready.gov's hurricane guidance says to include medication in a go-bag because you may not have access to usual supplies for days or even weeks after a storm.

Build the kit around *your* household: children's doses if you have children, extra supplies for anyone with a chronic condition, and pets separately. Keep prescription medicines in original labeled containers unless your pharmacist or clinician has told you otherwise, and ask your clinician or pharmacist how to store temperature-sensitive medicine during an outage. ReadyGearHub's [Cold Hold](/apps/cold-hold/) app is a fridge-temperature aid; it is not a substitute for FDA or pharmacist guidance.

## What a storm-ready first aid kit usually contains

A pre-built kit is a practical starting point if it is organized so you can find things quickly. Family-oriented options include the [Adventure Medical Kits Family kit](/gear/amk-adventure-first-aid-family/), which AMK sizes for 1–4 people on a day outing and pairs with a children's care booklet and a removable mini kit, or a larger kit such as the [Mountain Series Explorer](/gear/amk-mountain-series-explorer/) or a home/shop kit such as the [First Aid Only 50-person kit](/gear/first-aid-only-50-person-272/). A compact waterproof kit such as the [Ultralight/Watertight .9](/gear/amk-ultralight-watertight-9/) can ride in a go-bag. Whatever you buy, open it before storm season and compare it with the official contents list.

Typical categories, which you should confirm against the Red Cross first-aid training materials and the kit's own list, include:

- **Gloves and protection:** disposable gloves, and a way to clean your hands
- **Bleeding and wound care:** adhesive bandages in several sizes, sterile gauze, a roller bandage, tape, antiseptic wipes, and a larger trauma pad if the kit includes one
- **Blisters, burns, and sprains:** moleskin or blister dressings, a burn dressing or gel if included, and an elastic bandage
- **Tools:** scissors, tweezers, a thermometer, and a first-aid reference card or booklet
- **Personal medicines:** any over-the-counter items your clinician has said are appropriate for your household, stored as labeled, plus your prescription medications

Add a [SAM Splint](/gear/sam-splint/) only if you know how to use it, or after you have been taught in a class. Do not practice new medical techniques for the first time during a storm.

## Bleeding-control and AED gear

Some households also keep a compact trauma pack, such as the [AMK Trauma Pak with QuikClot](/gear/amk-trauma-pak-quikclot/), or a larger trauma-capable kit such as the [My Medic MyFAK](/gear/mymedic-myfak/). These are for serious bleeding until professionals arrive. They are not a reason to delay calling 911, and they work best if someone in the house has taken bleeding-control training.

An AED, such as the [Philips HeartStart OnSite](/gear/philips-heartstart-onsite/), is a medical device for suspected sudden cardiac arrest. Philips designs the OnSite with voice prompts, but you should still get CPR and AED training, follow the device manual, and check local rules for owning and placing an AED. Call 911 first.

## Where to keep it, and how to maintain it

- Keep one kit with your home storm supplies, in a spot you can reach in the dark, and a smaller kit in the car if you may have to evacuate.
- Check expiration dates on medicines, wipes, and sterile items on a schedule, and restock after you use anything.
- Include a written medication list and emergency contacts; the Red Cross specifically calls out copies of medical information in a survival kit.
- In a flood, move the kit high. In a power outage, a flashlight (not just a phone) belongs next to the kit.

If someone is badly hurt, has chest pain, trouble breathing, uncontrolled bleeding, or a suspected head, neck, or spine injury, call 911. A well-stocked kit supports care. It does not replace it.
