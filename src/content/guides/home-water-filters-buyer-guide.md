A home water filter can make tap water taste better, and the right one can cut specific contaminants like lead or PFAS. But filters are not all the same, and the box rarely tells you the whole story. This guide walks through how to figure out what you actually need, which type of filter fits, and how to read the label so you buy a filter for the right job.

One thing up front: a filter is a daily-water tool. It is not your emergency water plan. For storm prep, you still want stored water. Our [emergency water storage guide](/guides/emergency-water-storage/) covers how much to keep and how to store it.

## Step 1: Find out what is in your water

Before you shop, find out what you are filtering. If you get water from a city or water district, your utility has to send you a yearly water quality report, called a Consumer Confidence Report. The [EPA explains these reports](https://www.epa.gov/ccr) and how to find yours. Most utilities post it online; search your utility's name plus "water quality report."

The report lists what was detected, how much, and the legal limit. Look for:

- **Lead.** Lead usually comes from old pipes or fixtures in a home, not the treatment plant, so a city report may not tell you what comes out of your own tap. If you live in an older home, a home test is worth it.
- **PFAS.** In April 2024 the EPA set the first national drinking water limits for several PFAS chemicals. The [EPA's PFAS page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas) explains the rule and the timeline for water systems.
- **Disinfectants and their byproducts.** Chlorine or chloramine keeps city water safe, but some people don't like the taste.
- **Hardness.** Hard water is mostly a scale and soap problem, not a health one. Most drinking-water filters are not water softeners.

If you are on a private well, nobody tests it for you. Test it yourself, especially after flooding.

## Step 2: Learn the certification label

This is the part most people skip, and it matters most. "Removes 99% of contaminants" on a box means little by itself. Independent certification tells you a filter was tested for a named contaminant.

[NSF explains the main standards](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/nsf-ansi-42-53-and-401-filtration-systems-standards) in plain terms:

- **NSF/ANSI 42** covers taste and odor items like chlorine and particulates. Good for flavor, not health claims.
- **NSF/ANSI 53** covers health-related contaminants. Lead, some cysts and VOCs, and PFOA/PFOS claims live here.
- **NSF/ANSI 58** is for reverse osmosis systems.
- **NSF/ANSI 401** covers trace "emerging" compounds such as some medications and pesticides.

Here is the catch: a certification number alone is not enough. A filter certified to Standard 53 might be certified only for lead, not PFAS. Check which contaminants are listed for that exact model. NSF also notes that [to make a PFAS reduction claim](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/pfas-drinking-water), a filter must reduce PFAS below 20 parts per trillion in testing, and that boiling water does not reduce PFAS.

Other labs, like WQA and IAPMO, certify to the same standards. Whatever the lab, look the exact model up in the certifier's listing rather than trusting a badge on a product photo.

## Step 3: Pick the type that fits your home

### Pitchers and bottles

**Best for:** renters, small households, or trying filtration before a bigger purchase.

Pitchers need no tools and no plumbing. The trade-off is volume: you fill, wait and refill, and cartridges need frequent replacement. Pitchers like the [Epic Pure XP](/gear/epic-pure-xp-pitcher/) and [Epic Nano XP](/gear/epic-nano-xp-pitcher/) are sold for drinking water at the counter. Read the maker's page for the specific contaminants each model is tested for, and how many gallons a cartridge lasts.

A filter bottle such as the [Epic OG Ultimate Travel Bottle](/gear/epic-og-ultimate-travel-bottle/) does the same job on the go.

### Faucet-mount filters

**Best for:** filtered water on tap without plumbing work.

A faucet filter like the [iSpring DF1](/gear/ispring-df1/) screws onto most standard faucets and has a switch for filtered or unfiltered water. They are cheap to start with, but they don't fit pull-down or some designer faucets, and the cartridges are small.

### Under-sink filters

**Best for:** families who drink and cook with a lot of tap water.

Under-sink filters connect to the cold-water line and either use your existing faucet or add a small one. The [Epic Smart Shield](/gear/epic-smart-shield/) connects to the cold line and uses the existing faucet, per Epic, and the [Smart Shield Max](/gear/epic-smart-shield-max/) is Epic's three-stage version. Installation is usually a basic DIY job with a wrench, but check the instructions before you start.

### Reverse osmosis (RO)

**Best for:** when your report or test shows something a carbon filter won't handle, or you want the broadest reduction at one tap.

RO pushes water through a very fine membrane. It reduces a wide range of dissolved substances, but it has trade-offs:

- It sends some water down the drain as part of normal operation. Each maker lists its own ratio.
- Traditional systems store water in a tank under the sink, which takes space.
- RO also removes minerals. Some systems add a remineralization stage for taste.

The [iSpring RCC7](/gear/ispring-rcc7/) is a classic 5-stage under-sink RO with a storage tank, and the [iSpring RO500](/gear/ispring-ro500/) is a tankless design listed at 500 gallons per day. If you rent, a countertop RO unit that plugs in and needs no plumbing may be the better fit.

### Whole-house filters

**Best for:** treating every tap and shower at once, usually for sediment, chlorine taste and odor.

A whole-house system installs on the main water line where it enters your home. The [iSpring WGB21B](/gear/ispring-wgb21b/) is a 2-stage sediment and carbon system, and the [WGB32B](/gear/ispring-wgb32b/) adds a third stage with larger cartridges. Whole-house systems usually need a plumber, a shutoff, and room near the main line. Many people pair a whole-house sediment and carbon system with an under-sink filter at the kitchen tap for drinking water.

You'll find more under-sink, RO and whole-house options from Aquasana, Waterdrop and others in the [Water & Air directory](/directory/water/) and the [Home & Kitchen directory](/directory/home/), plus gravity filters like the Big Berkey, which need no plumbing or power at all.

## Step 4: Think about outages and storms

A filter that needs water pressure stops being useful when the water stops. A few things to know before a storm:

- **Boil-water notices.** If your utility issues a boil-water notice, follow it. Unless a filter is specifically certified to remove bacteria and viruses, don't assume it makes the water safe. [Ready.gov's water page](https://www.ready.gov/water) covers how to make water safe in an emergency.
- **Powered systems.** Some RO units and countertop dispensers need electricity. Know whether yours does.
- **Gravity and pitcher filters** keep working in an outage, as long as you have water to pour in.
- **After flooding,** a whole-house filter can clog fast with sediment. Have spare cartridges and know where your shutoff valve is.
- **Stored water first.** In an emergency, your stored water is the plan. A filter is a backup.

## Step 5: Budget for the whole life of the filter

The cartridge you buy later costs more over time than the unit you buy now. Before you buy, check:

1. **Cartridge life.** Makers list a gallon rating or a time limit. Use whichever comes first.
2. **Replacement cost and availability.** Look up the replacement filter price on the maker's page.
3. **What happens when it's overdue.** A worn-out filter stops doing its job. Set a phone reminder when you change a cartridge.

Any price on this site carries the date we checked it and can change. The "check current price" link on each product card takes you to the store page.

## A quick way to choose

- **Taste is the main issue:** pitcher or faucet filter certified to Standard 42 for chlorine.
- **Lead in an older home:** pitcher, faucet or under-sink filter certified to Standard 53 for lead.
- **PFAS in your report:** a filter certified to Standard 53 or 58 for PFOA/PFOS. Check the exact model listing.
- **Lots of dissolved substances or a broad concern:** under-sink RO.
- **Sediment, chlorine smell in showers, or a whole-home taste issue:** whole-house sediment and carbon, plus a drinking-water filter at the kitchen tap.

## The bottom line

Start with your water report, match the filter to the contaminants you actually have, and confirm the certification for that exact model. Then budget for cartridges and keep a supply of stored water for emergencies, because no filter works without water to filter.
