Your phone buzzes: **Winter Storm Watch.** A day later it says **Warning.** Then there's an **Advisory** for the next county over. These words mean different things, and knowing the difference tells you how much time you have and what to do with it. Here's a plain-English guide based on National Weather Service (NWS) and Ready.gov definitions.

## The short version

The NWS sorts its winter alerts into three levels:

| Alert level | NWS shorthand | What it means | What you do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Watch** | "Be prepared" | Conditions are favorable for a significant winter storm. It's possible, not certain. | Get ready now while stores are open and roads are clear. |
| **Warning** | "Take action" | Hazardous winter weather is happening or about to. | Finish prep, get home, and stay put. |
| **Advisory** | "Be aware" | Wintry weather is expected, but it shouldn't reach warning level. | Use caution, especially on the roads. |

Ready.gov adds the timing: **Winter Storm Watches are usually issued 12 to 48 hours before a storm**, and **Winter Storm Warnings usually 12 to 24 hours before.**

## Why a "small" storm can get a warning in the South

This trips people up. The NWS says each local office issues alerts **based on local criteria**, and gives this example: the amount of snow that triggers a Winter Storm Warning in the Northern Plains is typically much higher than the amount needed in the Southeast.

So if you live in Mississippi, Alabama, or Louisiana and see a warning for what sounds like a modest amount of snow or ice, take it seriously. The criteria are set for *your* area: your roads, your trees, and how your community handles winter weather.

## The winter alerts you're most likely to see

**Winter Storm Watch.** Per the NWS, it's issued when conditions are favorable for a significant winter storm: heavy sleet, heavy snow, an ice storm, or a mix. *Your move:* this is your prep window. Use our [48-hour ice storm prep checklist](/guides/ice-storm-prep-checklist-south/).

**Winter Storm Warning.** The NWS issues this for a significant winter weather event involving snow, ice, sleet, blowing snow, or a combination, and says travel will become difficult or impossible in some situations. Ready.gov's advice: **if you are under a Winter Storm Warning, find shelter right away.**

**Ice Storm Warning.** The NWS says these are usually issued for **ice accumulation of around 1/4 inch or more**. That much ice makes travel dangerous or impossible and is likely to cause snapped power lines and falling tree branches. *Your move:* expect a power outage. Charge everything, set up your warm room, and read [what to do when the power goes out in an ice storm](/guides/power-outage-ice-storm-mississippi/).

**Winter Weather Advisory.** Snow, ice, sleet, or a mix is expected but shouldn't be bad enough to meet warning criteria, according to the NWS. *Your move:* plan for slick roads. NWS safety pages note that bridges and overpasses freeze before other surfaces, and that black ice can be nearly invisible.

**Blizzard Warning.** For strong, frequent wind gusts with falling or blowing snow that cuts visibility to under a quarter mile for hours. It's rare in the Deep South. The NWS advice is not to travel.

**Freeze Watch / Freeze Warning.** These are about below-freezing temperatures rather than precipitation. In the South, a Freeze Warning is your cue to protect **pipes, plants, pets, and people**. See [how to keep pipes from freezing](/guides/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/).

**Extreme Cold Watch / Warning and Cold Weather Advisory.** These cover dangerously cold temperatures and wind chills. The NWS has been moving away from the older "Wind Chill" alert names, so you may still see those on older pages. The current NWS alert list includes Extreme Cold Watch, Extreme Cold Warning, and Cold Weather Advisory. *Your move:* limit time outside, dress in layers, and watch for frostbite and hypothermia.

## What to do at each level

### When a Watch is issued

- [ ] Check your supplies: water, food that needs no cooking, medications, pet food, flashlights, and batteries.
- [ ] Test your smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. Ready.gov recommends both with battery backups.
- [ ] Charge phones, power banks, and any power station.
- [ ] Fill your gas tank. Ready.gov recommends keeping a full tank in winter.
- [ ] Do your pipe prep: drain hoses and insulate exposed lines.
- [ ] Make sure you have a way to get alerts if cell service drops (see below).

### When a Warning is issued

- [ ] Get home and plan to stay there. Ready.gov says to stay off roads if at all possible.
- [ ] Bring pets inside.
- [ ] Set faucets to drip if a hard freeze is expected, and open cabinet doors on outside walls.
- [ ] Pick the room you'll keep warm if the power goes out.
- [ ] Put flashlights and headlamps where you can find them in the dark.

### When an Advisory is issued

- [ ] Allow extra time, or skip the trip.
- [ ] Clear your whole car, roof included, before driving.
- [ ] Wear shoes with good grip, or slip-on ice cleats, for steps and driveways.

## How to get alerts when the power and cell service are out

Ready.gov lists several ways to get emergency alerts: sign up for your community's warning system, the **Emergency Alert System (EAS)**, and **NOAA Weather Radio**. Phones also get Wireless Emergency Alerts, but a long outage can knock out cell service or drain your battery. ## Bottom line

A **watch** means get ready, a **warning** means act now, and an **advisory** means be careful. In the South, where alert criteria are set lower, it pays to act on the watch instead of waiting for the warning, because by then store shelves and roads may already be a problem.
