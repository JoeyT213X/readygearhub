A portable power station is a big rechargeable battery with outlets. Unlike a gas generator, it's silent, produces no fumes, and is safe to use indoors. That makes it one of the most popular pieces of outage gear. There are dozens of models, though, so here's how to choose without paying for more than you need.

## Step 1: Know the two numbers that matter

- **Capacity, in watt-hours (Wh):** how much energy the battery holds. Think of it as the size of the gas tank.
- **Output, in watts (W):** how much power it can deliver at once. Think of it as horsepower. This decides *which* devices it can run.

Your device needs to fit under the **output** limit, and the **capacity** decides *how long* it runs.

## Step 2: List what you actually need to run

Look at the label or power brick on each device for its watts. Typical examples:

| Device | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Phone charging | 5–20 W |
| LED lamp or lantern | 5–15 W |
| Wi-Fi router and modem | 10–25 W |
| Laptop | 30–90 W |
| CPAP (without heated humidifier) | 30–60 W (check your machine) |
| Full-size refrigerator | 100–200 W while running, with higher startup surges |
| Space heater | 750–1,500 W |

These are ballpark figures. Always check your own device labels.

**Simple sizing math:** add up the watts of what you'll run at the same time, then multiply by the hours you want. Then add about 15–20% because of conversion losses.

> Example: router (20 W) + two lamps (20 W) + phone charging (20 W) = 60 W. Running that for 10 hours = 600 Wh. Add 20%, and you're looking at roughly **700 Wh or more** of capacity.

## Step 3: Rough size categories

- **Under 500 Wh:** phones, lights, a router, and a laptop for an evening. Easy to carry.
- **About 1,000 Wh:** the sweet spot for most households. It covers essentials overnight and can run a fridge in short bursts. Most of the models in our [Power Outage Kits](/power-outage-kits/) lane are around this size.
- **1,500–3,000+ Wh:** for longer outages, medical equipment, or running a fridge for longer. Heavier, and often expandable with add-on batteries.

## Step 4: Check the battery type

Most newer models use **LiFePO4 (LFP)** batteries, which are rated by manufacturers for many more charge cycles than older lithium-ion types. If you plan to use it often, like camping or daily solar charging, LFP is worth it.

## Step 5: Think about recharging

During a multi-day outage, how you recharge matters as much as capacity:

- **Wall charging speed:** many new models recharge from an outlet in about an hour, which is handy between rolling outages.
- **Solar input:** check the maximum solar watts and connector type, and buy a panel that matches. Real-world solar output is usually lower than the panel's rating, especially in winter.
- **Car charging:** a slow but useful backup.

## Step 6: Don't skip these details

- **Pure sine wave AC output** is safer for sensitive electronics and medical devices. Most major brands have it.
- **UPS or pass-through mode** lets some units switch over automatically when the power cuts out, useful for CPAPs and routers. Check the manufacturer's stated switchover time.
- **Weight:** a 1,000 Wh unit often weighs 20–30 lb. Make sure whoever needs to move it can.
- **Warranty and support:** stick with established brands that publish clear warranty terms.

## What a power station can't do well

It isn't a whole-home solution. Electric heat, electric ovens, well pumps, and central AC draw too much for most portable units. For those, people use a properly installed generator with a transfer switch, operated outdoors and at least 20 feet from the home as the CDC recommends.

## Bottom line

For most homes, a **1,000 Wh-class LFP power station plus one compatible solar panel** covers the essentials through a typical outage.
