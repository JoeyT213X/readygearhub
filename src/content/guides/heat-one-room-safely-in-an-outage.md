When the heat goes out in winter, the safest plan is usually not to heat the whole house. It is to keep **one occupied room** warm enough, stay dry, and avoid carbon monoxide and fire. Most outage injuries in this situation come from unsafe heat sources, not from a slightly chilly room.

This guide is about doing that safely. It is not a reason to improvise with equipment that was never meant for indoor use.

This page is about **heaters and heat sources**. For staying warm with layers, blankets and warm food when you have no heater at all, read [how to stay warm when the power goes out](/guides/how-to-stay-warm-without-power/).

## First: what you should never use for indoor heat

Carbon monoxide (CO) is colorless and odorless, and it can kill quickly. [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather) and the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) are explicit:

- **Never** use a generator, gas or charcoal grill, camp stove, or similar device inside the home, in a basement, in a garage, or near windows.
- **Never** heat the house with a gas stovetop or oven. Ready.gov notes that this also raises the risk of fires, burns, and appliance damage.
- **Never** bring an outdoor-rated patio heater, construction heater, or other outdoor combustion device indoors.
- Generators belong **outdoors and at least 20 feet** from windows, doors, and attached garages. Follow the manufacturer's ventilation instructions. See the [Honda EU2200i](/gear/honda-eu2200i/) listing for an example of how that safety language appears on a real generator page.

If anyone in the house feels dizzy, nauseated, or has a headache, get to fresh air and seek medical help. Those can be signs of CO poisoning.

## Make the room hold heat before you add any heat source

The [CDC's winter-storm guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) on conserving heat is straightforward:

- Close off unused rooms.
- Stuff towels or rags in the cracks under doors.
- Close drapes or cover windows with blankets at night, and avoid opening doors unnecessarily.

A small interior room, or a bedroom on the leeward side of the house, usually holds heat better than a big room with many windows. An [indoor window film kit](/gear/3m-indoor-window-insulator-kit/) can cut drafts if you installed it before the storm.

Then add insulation around people, which does not require electricity:

- Dress in layers. The CDC notes that wool, silk, or polypropylene hold more body heat than cotton when you need a base layer, and that a hat and dry socks matter.
- Use extra blankets, a [sleeping bag](/gear/teton-sports-celsius-regular-0/), and a [foam pad](/gear/therm-a-rest-z-lite-sol/) so body heat is not lost to a cold floor.
- Eat regular meals and drink warm, non-alcoholic, non-caffeinated fluids if your doctor has not restricted them. The CDC warns that alcohol and caffeine can make the body lose heat faster.

Watch for hypothermia: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, or drowsiness. A body temperature below 95°F is a medical emergency, according to Ready.gov and the CDC.

## If you still have household power: electric space heaters

An electric space heater, such as the [Vornado AVH10](/gear/vornado-avh10/), can warm one room **only if the outlet still has power** (or if a properly installed, outdoor generator can support the heater's draw). A typical portable energy reserve cannot run a 750–1,500 watt heater for long, which is why our [energy-reserve guide](/guides/how-to-pick-an-energy-reserve/) advises against it.

If you do use a space heater, follow the manufacturer and the CDC:

- Choose a heater with an automatic shut-off, as the CDC recommends for electric models.
- Keep it **at least 3 feet** from drapes, furniture, bedding, and anything else that can burn. Never cover a heater.
- Never place a heater on furniture or near water.
- Do not run the cord under a rug, and avoid extension cords.
- Do not use a heater with a damaged cord or one that sparks.
- Never leave children unattended near a heater, and do not leave a heater running in a room with no one awake.

Plug the heater directly into a wall outlet, keep a working smoke alarm nearby, and turn the heater off when you sleep unless the manufacturer's instructions clearly allow otherwise.

## Fuel-burning heaters: only if they are rated for indoor use

Some catalytic or propane devices, including indoor-rated models like the [Mr. Heater Portable Buddy](/gear/mr-heater-portable-buddy/), are sold for indoor use under strict conditions. That does **not** make every propane heater safe indoors, and it does not remove the carbon monoxide risk.

If you use any combustion heater or a vented fireplace or wood stove:

- Use it only if the manufacturer rates it for indoor use, and follow every ventilation, clearance, and fuel instruction.
- The CDC says combustion heaters must be properly vented to the outside and must not leak exhaust into the room. Use only the fuel the heater is designed for.
- Have a working, battery-powered [carbon monoxide alarm](/gear/kidde-kn-cob-lp2/) on every level and near bedrooms. The [Eton Blackout Buddy CO](/gear/eton-blackout-buddy-co/) is an example of a nightlight that also includes CO detection, but it is not a substitute for placing alarms the way the manufacturer instructs.
- Open a window slightly if the manufacturer requires it. "Ventless" is not the same as "no oxygen use and no exhaust."
- Never fall asleep next to a fuel-burning heater unless the instructions expressly allow overnight use.

If you cannot meet those conditions, do not use the heater. Extra blankets are safer than a closed-room combustion source. For generator placement and other outage CO rules, see [Carbon Monoxide Safety During a Power Outage](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-during-an-outage/).

## When the house is no longer safe to stay in

If indoor temperatures keep falling, if anyone is medically vulnerable, or if you smell exhaust or the CO alarm sounds, leave. Many communities open warming centers during long winter outages; listen to local officials, a weather radio, the [What's happening now](/#live-status) board, and [live alerts](/alerts/) for locations. You can also follow occasional official-source notes on [Local & community safety updates](/updates/). Mississippi readers can find official county emergency-management contacts, shelter sources, and utility outage maps on our [Mississippi local resources](/local/mississippi/) pages, including [Hinds](/local/mississippi/hinds/), [Madison](/local/mississippi/madison/), and [Rankin](/local/mississippi/rankin/) in the Jackson metro, and [Harrison](/local/mississippi/harrison/), [Hancock](/local/mississippi/hancock/), and [Jackson County](/local/mississippi/jackson/) on the Gulf Coast. Go to a friend or relative with heat if you can travel safely, or wait for a plow or sand truck if the roads are ice-covered.

## Common questions

**Is a "ventless" propane heater automatically safe in a closed room?**
No. Use a combustion heater only if the manufacturer rates it for indoor use, follow every ventilation and clearance instruction, and keep a working battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm in the room. "Ventless" is not the same as no exhaust.

**What should I never use to heat one room?**
The CDC says never to use a generator, grill, camp stove, or charcoal indoors, and never to heat the house with a gas oven. Details are in [Carbon Monoxide Safety During a Power Outage](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-during-an-outage/).

**When is it time to leave instead of trying to heat one room?**
If indoor temperatures keep falling, if anyone is medically vulnerable, if you smell exhaust, or if a CO alarm sounds, leave for a warming center or a place with heat, as local officials announce.
