A portable generator can keep a fridge, lights, a well pump or medical equipment running through a long outage. It can also kill people in minutes if it is run in the wrong place, or injure utility workers if it is plugged into a dryer outlet. This guide walks through a safe hookup: the generator stays outdoors, the house connection goes through a listed transfer switch or interlock, and propane tanks are placed and installed by the rules that apply to them.

If you would rather avoid fuel and exhaust altogether, start with [generator vs. Energy Reserve](/guides/generator-vs-energy-reserve/) and [carbon monoxide safety during an outage](/guides/carbon-monoxide-safety-during-an-outage/). The products and hookup parts mentioned here live in our [Backup Power & Propane](/directory/backup-power-propane/) directory.

This is not an installation manual, and it is not a substitute for the National Electrical Code, NFPA 58, or a permit inspection. Use a licensed electrician for anything that ties into house wiring, and a qualified gas installer for permanent or larger propane work.

## Keep the generator outside, at least 20 feet from the house

[Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages) says to never run a generator inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, or other enclosed or partly enclosed space, even with doors and windows open, and to keep it at least 20 feet from the home, with exhaust pointed away.

The [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Guides/Carbon-Monoxide-Home/Generators-and-Engine-Driven-Tools) gives the same 20-foot rule. In its [May 27, 2026 hurricane-season generator advisory](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2026/CPSC-Warns-of-Generator-Carbon-Monoxide-and-Fire-Hazards-Ahead-of-Hurricane-Season) and its [March 16, 2026 winter-weather safety release](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2026/CPSC-Issues-Winter-Weather-Safety-Tips-to-Prevent-Fires-and-Carbon-Monoxide-Poisoning), CPSC also says not to run a generator on a porch or in a carport, to point the exhaust away from the house and other building entrances, and to close windows and seal vents that sit near the generator or in the path of its exhaust. Opening a door or window is not enough ventilation. Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless, and CPSC warns that it can incapacitate a person before they notice nausea, dizziness or weakness.

Place [battery-powered carbon monoxide alarms, or plug-in alarms with battery backup](/gear/kidde-plugin-battery-co/), on each level and near bedrooms, as the manufacturer instructs. Test them. A working alarm is a backup, not permission to pull the generator closer to the house.

If it is raining or snowing, read the generator's own manual. CPSC notes that some maker instructions may include an [NFPA-rated noncombustible generator tent](/gear/gentent-open-frame/), or may tell you to wait until the weather passes. A purpose-built canopy is not a reason to set the unit on the porch.

## Do not backfeed the house

The only safe way to feed house circuits from a portable generator is a listed transfer switch, a listed generator interlock kit for your exact panel, or a home-backup kit the Energy Reserve maker sells for that job. [OSHA's fact sheet on grounding portable generators](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/GROUNDING_PORT_GENERATOR.pdf) is plain on this point: never attach a portable generator directly to the electrical system of a house, office or trailer unless the generator has a properly installed open-transition transfer switch. The transfer switch must be approved for the use and installed, in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, by a qualified electrician.

Backfeeding means plugging a generator into a dryer outlet or a wall receptacle with a "suicide cord" so power flows backward through the panel. It can energize utility lines and kill a lineman, and it can also ruin the generator and the house wiring.

[Mississippi Power's electric service handbook](https://www.mississippipower.com/content/dam/mississippi-power/pdfs/business/electric-service-handbook/genral-information/ElectricServiceHandbook-2021.pdf) (2021) tells customers the same thing in local terms. Standby generators must be installed to the National Electrical Code and local codes. Customers have to provide an adequately sized double-throw switch that opens all ungrounded conductors from the normal supply before the emergency supply is connected, and the other way around. Power from a standby generator must never be supplied to another premise, because of the danger of back-feeding the distribution system. If you want a generator that runs in parallel with the utility, that is a different, pre-approved interconnection, not a portable hookup.

In practice, that usually means one of these, sized and listed for your panel and your generator:

- A manual transfer switch kit, such as a [Reliance Controls Pro/Tran 10-circuit, 30-amp kit](/gear/reliance-31410crk/) or a [Generac HomeLink 30-amp manual transfer switch](/gear/generac-homelink-30/).
- A manufacturer interlock kit that is listed for your exact load center, such as the [Square D HOMCGK2C](/gear/square-d-homcgk2c/) for certain Homeline panels. An interlock is not a universal part. The electrician has to match the kit to the panel model.
- For an Energy Reserve, the transfer kit that maker sells for that model, such as [BLUETTI's Apex 300 home integration kit](/gear/bluetti-apex-300-home-integration-kit/) or [Anker SOLIX's 10-circuit switch for the F3800](/gear/anker-solix-f3800-10c-backup-kit/).

Local building permits can apply. Ask the electrician what the city or county requires, and see the [Mississippi State Board of Contractors FAQ](https://www.msboc.us/general-info/frequently-asked-questions/) if you need a starting point on licensing. Do not open a panel yourself to "just try" an interlock.

## Grounding depends on how you use the generator

Grounding is easy to get wrong, and a short hardware-store rod is not automatically the right answer. OSHA's fact sheet explains two different cases.

If the generator is only powering tools or appliances that are plugged into receptacles on the generator itself, and the metal parts of the generator are bonded to its frame, OSHA says the frame may serve as the ground and a driven ground rod is not required under that condition.

If the generator is feeding a structure through a transfer switch, OSHA says it must be connected to a grounding electrode system, such as a driven ground rod. Confirm the details in the generator manual, the transfer-switch instructions, and Article 250 of the National Electrical Code, and have a qualified electrician test the connections. OSHA also recommends heavy-duty, three-wire outdoor cords and GFCI protection as the manufacturer instructs, and says not to use damaged cords.

For a small outage, the simplest safe option is still to plug a fridge, lights or medical device into the generator with a proper outdoor-rated cord, while the generator sits 20 feet from the house. The transfer switch is what you add when you want selected house circuits to come on without running cords through a window.

## Propane: tanks, hoses, and Mississippi L.C. Gas rules

A dual-fuel or tri-fuel generator can run on propane when gasoline is short. A [20-pound cylinder](/gear/flame-king-20lb/) is a common starting size. Use a hose and regulator that are meant for that generator or appliance, such as a [Champion generator hose kit](/gear/champion-12ft-propane-hose/). Check fittings for leaks with soapy water, never with a flame. Store cylinders outdoors and upright.

Distance matters, and it depends on the container. The [NFPA 58 fact sheet on placing LP-gas containers](https://www.nfpa.org/-/media/project/storefront/catalog/files/code-or-topic-fact-sheets/lpgascontainerfactsheet.pdf) is the source to use, not a guess. Among the figures it states clearly: keep a cylinder’s relief valve at least 3 feet from a building opening below it, and at least 5 feet from an exterior ignition source and from openings into direct-vent appliances (and 10 feet if the cylinder is filled on site from a cargo tank, measured at the filling connection and vent valve). Underground or mounded ASME tanks have a 10-foot minimum to a building or to a property line that can be built upon. For some larger aboveground ASME tanks, the fact sheet discusses a 25-foot table distance, with a 10-foot option for a single tank of 1,200 gallons or less under the conditions it lists. Read the table for your exact tank; do not scale a 20-pound cylinder rule up to a 100-pound or ASME tank on your own.

In Mississippi, propane work is not a casual weekend project. The [Mississippi Insurance Department's Liquefied Compressed Gas Division](https://apps.mid.ms.gov/sfm/liquefied-compressed-gas.aspx) enforces the state's L.C. Gas laws and inspects installations. Its official [installation report](https://www.mid.ms.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/lcgasinstallreport.pdf) must be completed and emailed to the division within 15 days after an installation, replacement or repair. The same form states that no distributor or other person may fill an L.C. Gas container unless the installation has first been inspected and approved by an L.C. Gas inspector, or was installed by a person who holds a valid installer's certificate from the L.C. Gas Division. [Mississippi Code § 75-57-47](https://law.justia.com/codes/mississippi/title-75/chapter-57/in-general/section-75-57-47/) also requires the installer to notify the State Liquefied Compressed Gas Board, in writing, within 15 days after the work is finished.

That filing and fill rule is the licensed-installer case for a permanent or larger tank. A simple, portable cylinder hooked to a generator or appliance with a listed hose is a different situation, but the cylinder still belongs outdoors and upright, at a safe distance. Hire an installer who holds that certificate for anything you set, bury, or pipe as a permanent system. Do not expect a refill of a new permanent tank if the installation has not been reported and approved.

## An Energy Reserve is a different kind of hookup

A home-backup Energy Reserve, such as the [BLUETTI Apex 300](/directory/power/#bluetti-apex-300) or the [Anker SOLIX F3800 Plus](/gear/anker-solix-f3800-plus/), stores electricity in a battery and can sit indoors. It has no exhaust. The transfer kit still has to be installed correctly, and a licensed electrician should do that work. If you add a fuel-powered companion generator to charge the battery, that generator still belongs outdoors, 20 feet from the house.

For many households, an Energy Reserve is the simpler path for lights, phones, a router, a fan or a CPAP. A portable generator is the path for larger loads and longer outages, if — and only if — it is placed and connected the way the sources above describe.

## A simple order of work

1. Decide which loads actually have to run, and whether a smaller outdoor generator, an Energy Reserve, or both is the better fit.
2. Hire a licensed electrician to specify and install a listed transfer switch, inlet and cord, or a listed interlock for your exact panel. Ask about local permits.
3. Place the generator's outdoor pad at least 20 feet from the house, with exhaust pointed away from openings. Install CO alarms with battery backup before you ever start the unit.
4. If you will run propane, use the right hose and regulator, keep cylinders outdoors, and use an L.C. Gas installer for anything beyond a simple, portable cylinder hookup.
5. Do a dry run on a clear day: start the generator outdoors, transfer the circuits, and confirm the loads you care about actually come on.

## The bottom line

The safe picture is simple, even if the parts list is not. The generator stays outside and far from the house. The house connection goes through equipment a licensed electrician installs. Propane tanks stay outdoors and, in Mississippi, larger or permanent work goes through a certified L.C. Gas installer who files the report. When you want less fuel and no exhaust, use an Energy Reserve and still treat the home hookup as electrical work, not a DIY cord.
