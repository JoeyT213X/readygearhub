A tornado watch and a tornado warning are not the same thing, and in Mississippi that difference matters in more than one season. The National Weather Service in Jackson notes that November marks the peak of the state's second severe-weather season, and that it ranks as the third most active month for tornadoes in Mississippi, just behind March ([NWS Jackson, November 29–30, 2010 event summary](https://www.weather.gov/jan/2010_11_29_30_severe_weather_front_page)). On a separate preparedness page, NWS Jackson describes two peaks in tornadoes across Mississippi and says November tornadoes, based on the last 20 years of data it reviewed, strongly favor nighttime occurrence ([NWS Jackson: Fall Severe Weather Preparedness, Tornado Safety](https://www.weather.gov/jan/swpwfall_tornadoes)).

Night storms are harder to see. That is why a practiced safe place and a way to get alerts that does not depend only on a glance out the window matter here.

## The short version

| Alert | NWS shorthand | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tornado Watch** | "Be prepared" | Tornadoes are possible in and near the watch area. | Review your plan, check your safe place, and stay ready to move. |
| **Tornado Warning** | "Take action" | A tornado has been sighted or indicated by weather radar. There is imminent danger to life and property. | Go now to a sturdy building's safest interior room. |
| **Tornado Emergency** | "Seek shelter immediately" | The National Weather Service's highest tornado alert, used when a violent tornado poses a severe threat to life and property. | Get to the safest shelter you can, immediately. |

Those definitions follow the National Weather Service's [Understand Tornado Alerts](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww) page. NWS Jackson's local wording is the same idea in shorter form: a watch means conditions are favorable, and a warning means a tornado has been sighted or rotation has been detected on radar — take immediate action, and do not wait for visual confirmation ([NWS Jackson tornado safety](https://www.weather.gov/jan/swpwfall_tornadoes)).

## Why Mississippi and the Gulf South need a year-round plan

The National Weather Service says tornadoes can occur at any time of day or night and at any time of the year, and that they are most common in the Central Plains and the southeastern United States ([NWS: Tornado Safety](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado)). [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes) likewise notes that the Midwest and the Southeast have a greater risk.

For Mississippi specifically, use NWS Jackson's climatology, not a generic "spring only" calendar: a spring peak, and a second season that peaks in November. A watch or warning in late fall or at night is as serious as one in April.

Gulf Coast counties are covered by the National Weather Service New Orleans/Baton Rouge office. You can read that office at [weather.gov/lix](https://www.weather.gov/lix/). Inland and central Mississippi counties are often served by [NWS Jackson](https://www.weather.gov/jan/). If you live in [Harrison](/local/mississippi/harrison/), [Hancock](/local/mississippi/hancock/), or [Jackson County](/local/mississippi/jackson/), start with those local resource pages for official emergency-management contacts.

## What each alert means

**Tornado Watch.** The NWS says tornadoes are possible in and near the watch area. Watches are issued by the Storm Prediction Center, typically for a large area covering numerous counties or even states. Review and discuss your emergency plans, take inventory of your supplies, and check your safe room. Be ready to act quickly if a warning is issued or you suspect a tornado is approaching ([NWS: Understand Tornado Alerts](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww)).

**Tornado Warning.** The NWS says a tornado has been sighted or indicated by weather radar, and there is imminent danger to life and property. Warnings are issued by your local forecast office and typically cover a much smaller area, around the size of a city or small county ([NWS: Understand Tornado Alerts](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww)). This is not the time to confirm the storm with your own eyes. NWS Jackson says taking time to wait for visual confirmation can take time away from seeking proper shelter.

**Tornado Emergency.** The NWS describes this as its highest alert level, used when a violent tornado poses a severe threat to human life and property. Seek the safest location you can immediately, and keep following official updates ([NWS: Understand Tornado Alerts](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww)).

## What to do

[Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes) and the NWS agree on the core move: when a warning is in effect, go right away to a safe location you have already identified.

**In a sturdy home or small building.** Go to a basement, a safe room, or a small interior room or hallway on the lowest floor, away from windows. NWS Jackson also lists a closet or bathroom on the lowest level. Cover your head and neck. Ready.gov suggests putting materials such as furniture and blankets around or on top of you. NWS Jackson suggests a helmet if you have one, or a blanket, cushion, pillow, or mattress rather than only your hands ([NWS Jackson tornado safety](https://www.weather.gov/jan/swpwfall_tornadoes); [NWS: What to do During a Tornado](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during); [Ready.gov: Tornadoes](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes)).

**At school, work, a nursing home, hospital, or shopping center.** Follow the tornado drill and go to the pre-designated shelter. Interior hallways on the lowest floor are usually best. Stay away from windows and from large open rooms such as cafeterias, gymnasiums, dining halls, or auditoriums, which often have weakly supported roofs ([NWS: What to do During a Tornado](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during); [NWS Jackson tornado safety](https://www.weather.gov/jan/swpwfall_tornadoes)).

**Outside.** Seek shelter inside a sturdy building immediately. The NWS says sheds and storage facilities are not safe, and neither is a mobile home or a tent ([NWS: What to do During a Tornado](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during)).

**Afterward.** Pay attention to official alerts and local authorities, stay clear of fallen power lines, and save phone calls for emergencies when you can use texts instead ([Ready.gov: Tornadoes](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes); [NWS: After a Tornado](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-after)).

## Safe rooms, and what to do in a mobile home

[Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes) says to identify and practice going to a safe shelter such as a safe room built using FEMA criteria or a storm shelter built to ICC 500 standards. The next best protection is a small, interior, windowless room or basement on the lowest level of a sturdy building.

A mobile or manufactured home is not that sturdy shelter. The NWS warning advice is to leave a mobile home, a vehicle, or an outdoor location and move to the closest substantial shelter, and to protect yourself from flying debris ([NWS: Understand Tornado Alerts](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww)). NWS Jackson says the same: if you are in a mobile home or a vehicle, leave it and go to a substantial structure. If no shelter is nearby, lie flat in the nearest ditch, ravine, gully, or culvert with your hands shielding your head — and it notes that this may not be a good option if heavy rain is falling ([NWS Jackson tornado safety](https://www.weather.gov/jan/swpwfall_tornadoes)).

If you live in a mobile home, decide *before* the watch where you will go — a nearby sturdy building, a friend's house, or a community shelter announced by local officials. Mississippi shelter and safe-room openings reported to the state are listed by [MEMA](https://www.msema.org/get-assistance/disaster-resources/shelters-and-safe-rooms), and county pages such as [Harrison](/local/mississippi/harrison/), [Hancock](/local/mississippi/hancock/), and [Jackson County](/local/mississippi/jackson/) point to official local sources. Openings change; do not travel to a site unless officials say it is open.

## How to get the alert, including at night

Because November tornadoes in Mississippi often occur at night, do not rely on seeing the storm. Ready.gov lists several official channels ([Ready.gov: Emergency Alerts](https://www.ready.gov/alerts); [Ready.gov: Tornadoes](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes)):

- **Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA).** Short emergency alerts that authorized officials, including the National Weather Service, can send to WEA-enabled phones in a targeted area. They look like text messages but use a unique sound and vibration, are not affected by network congestion, and do not require a subscription. Ready.gov notes that children also receive alerts on WEA-enabled devices, and that parents should talk with them about what the alert means.
- **NOAA Weather Radio.** A nationwide network that broadcasts continuous weather information, including official watches and warnings, from the nearest National Weather Service office, 24 hours a day. A battery or hand-crank radio still works if the power is out. See the NWS [NOAA Weather Radio](https://www.weather.gov/nwr/) page, and options such as the [Midland ER310](/gear/midland-er310-crank-radio/), the [Midland WR400](/gear/midland-wr400/), or the [Eton American Red Cross FRX2](/gear/eton-red-cross-frx2/).
- **Emergency Alert System (EAS).** Official alerts carried on broadcast television and radio.
- **Community warning systems and outdoor sirens**, where your county or city offers them. Sign up if your community has a list, and learn what the siren tone means. County contacts are on our [Mississippi local resources](/local/mississippi/) pages.

## Kids, pets, and older adults

Build the plan around the people and animals who may need more time or help.

**Children.** Practice going to the safe place the same way you would a fire drill, and explain what a WEA on a phone sounds like ([Ready.gov: Emergency Alerts](https://www.ready.gov/alerts)). At school, they should follow the school's tornado drill ([NWS: What to do During a Tornado](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during)). NWS Jackson encourages teaching children the best shelter areas in your home.

**Pets.** Ready.gov says to include pets in your family's emergency plan ([Ready.gov: Tornadoes](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes); [Ready.gov: Prepare Your Pets](https://www.ready.gov/pets)). The NWS says not to forget pets if time allows when you take shelter at home ([NWS: What to do During a Tornado](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during)). Keep a carrier or leash where you can reach them quickly, and do not spend warning time searching the yard. Our [Collar Card](/apps/collar-card/) app can store a simple pet profile for a go-bag.

**Older adults and anyone who needs extra help.** [Ready.gov's older-adult guidance](https://www.ready.gov/older-adults) calls for a support network, a practiced plan, and a kit that includes medicines and medical supplies. Make sure at least one person in that network has an extra key, knows where supplies are, and knows how to help. If stairs or mobility make a last-second move hard, identify the safest reachable place in advance and give yourselves time when a watch is issued. Our [Winter Emergency Kit for Elderly Parents](/guides/winter-emergency-kit-for-elderly-parents/) guide is written for cold-weather outages, but the check-in and medication-planning steps still help. ## If you are in a car

The NWS says being in a vehicle during a tornado is not safe. The best course of action is to drive to the closest shelter. If you cannot make it, either get down in the car and cover your head, or abandon the car and seek shelter in a low-lying area such as a ditch or ravine ([NWS: What to do During a Tornado](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during)). Ready.gov adds more detail: if the tornado is visible, far away, and traffic is light, you may be able to drive out of its path by moving at right angles to it; if you are caught by extreme winds or flying debris, park out of traffic lanes, stay in the car with the seat belt on, and keep your head down below the windows. Avoid seeking shelter under bridges ([Ready.gov: Tornadoes](https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes)). NWS Jackson also warns against staying in the vehicle when a substantial structure is available.

## Common questions

**Is a severe thunderstorm warning the same as a tornado warning?**
No. A tornado warning means a tornado has been sighted or indicated by radar and you should take shelter now. Treat a tornado warning as an immediate action, not as a regular thunderstorm update ([NWS: Understand Tornado Alerts](https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-ww)).

**Should I open windows?**
No. NWS Jackson says to stay away from windows and not to bother opening them. Opening windows will not protect the structure and wastes time you should use to get to shelter ([NWS Jackson tornado safety](https://www.weather.gov/jan/swpwfall_tornadoes)).
