Most winters in the South bring a few cold mornings and nothing more. Then every few years a storm drops freezing rain on trees and power lines, the roads glaze over, and a lot of us find out at the same time that our houses, cars, and plans were built for heat, not ice. This guide pulls everything together in one place: what to do before the storm, how to keep the house and the people in it safe while the power is out, and what to check once it's over.

## Why winter storms hit the South differently

A Southern winter storm usually isn't about deep snow. It's about ice. The [NWS explains](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-ice-frost) that heavy ice can bring down trees and topple utility poles and communication towers, and that it can disrupt power and communications for days while utilities repair the damage. Even a thin glaze makes roads, steps, and driveways dangerous, and bridges and overpasses freeze before other surfaces.

A few things make that worse down here:

- **We don't have the equipment.** Southern cities and counties typically have much less snow and ice equipment than places where snow is routine, so roads can stay iced over until temperatures rise.
- **Our houses are built for summer.** Many Southern homes have pipes in crawl spaces, attics, and exterior walls with little insulation, and many rely on electric heat. When the power goes out, the heat goes out with it.
- **Lots of trees, lots of overhead lines.** Pines and hardwoods loaded with ice drop limbs onto lines across wide areas, especially in rural electric co-op territory.
- **Little practice.** When a storm comes only every few years, most households don't have a routine, a kit, or a habit of checking alerts. That's what this guide is for.
- **Rural water and long drives.** Small water systems can lose pressure or issue boil-water notices, and the nearest open store, pharmacy, or warming center may be a long, icy drive away.

The good news: almost everything that keeps you safe can be done in a weekend before the cold arrives, and most of it costs little or nothing.

## Know the alerts and where to watch them

The NWS issues winter alerts based on local criteria, and it notes that the amount of snow that triggers a Winter Storm Warning in the Southeast is typically much lower than in the Northern Plains ([NWS: winter watches, warnings and advisories](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-ww)). So when your local office issues one, take it seriously even if the totals sound small.

| Alert | What it means (per NWS) | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Winter Storm Watch** | Conditions are favorable for a significant winter storm: heavy sleet, heavy snow, ice storm, or a mix. | Finish your prep: water, food, medicine, fuel, cash, charged batteries. |
| **Winter Weather Advisory** | Wintry weather is expected but shouldn't reach warning level. | Expect slick roads. Plan around travel. |
| **Winter Storm Warning** | A significant winter weather event is expected, and travel will become difficult or impossible in some places. | Stay home if you can. Finish last-minute tasks before it starts. |
| **Ice Storm Warning** | Usually issued for ice accumulation of around 1/4 inch or more, which the NWS says will make travel dangerous or impossible and likely lead to snapped power lines and falling branches. | Expect outages. Charge everything, set up your warm room, and stay off the roads. |
| **Wind Chill Warning / Advisory** | Dangerously cold wind chills that can cause frostbite and hypothermia. | Limit time outside and cover exposed skin. Bring pets in. |

Ready.gov adds that Winter Storm Watches are usually issued 12 to 48 hours before a storm and Warnings usually 12 to 24 hours before ([Ready.gov: Winter Weather](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather)). That watch window is your prep window. For a deeper explanation of each alert, including freeze warnings, read [Winter Storm Watch vs. Warning: What Each Alert Means](/guides/winter-storm-watch-vs-warning/).

## Before the storm: a countdown

You don't need to do everything on this page the day before. Spread it out.

### Now, before cold weather (one-time setup)

- [ ] Install **carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup** on every level of the home and near sleeping areas, and test your smoke alarms. (Details in the [heating section](#heat-safely-co-alarms-generators-and-fuel-heaters).)
- [ ] Insulate exposed pipes and find your **main water shut-off**. (See [Protect your pipes](#protect-your-pipes).)
- [ ] Have your furnace, water heater, and any fuel-burning appliance serviced. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) recommends a yearly check by a qualified technician, and a yearly chimney check or cleaning.
- [ ] Build a basic kit. The free [Emergency Kit Builder](/tools/kit-builder/) turns Ready.gov's list into a printable checklist sized to your household.
- [ ] Write down important phone numbers on paper: utility outage line, county emergency management, doctors, pharmacy, vet, and family. A dead phone shouldn't mean a lost list.
- [ ] Make electronic and paper copies of IDs, insurance policies, and other key documents, and decide how you'll stay safer on public Wi-Fi if you have to leave home. (See [Protect your accounts and documents](#protect-your-accounts-and-documents).)
- [ ] If anyone in the home depends on powered medical equipment, talk to the doctor and call your power company now. (See [Medical needs](#medical-and-power-dependent-needs).)
- [ ] Put a car kit in each vehicle. (See [Vehicles](#vehicles-and-travel).)
- [ ] If you can't afford heating costs or weatherization, Ready.gov points to the **Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP)** for help.

### When a Winter Storm Watch is issued

- [ ] **Water:** FoodSafety.gov says to store at least 1 gallon per person per day, and per pet, for at least 3 days ([FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency)). Use the [Water Storage Calculator](/tools/water-calculator/) to get your household's number.
- [ ] **Food:** at least a 3-day supply that needs little or no cooking, water, or refrigeration, plus a manual can opener. Include baby food, special diets, and pet food.
- [ ] **Medicine:** refill prescriptions early, before roads close.
- [ ] **Fuel and cash:** fill the car's gas tank (pumps need electricity) and get some cash (card readers go down with the power).
- [ ] **Batteries and charging:** charge phones, power banks, laptops, and any portable power station. Check flashlight and radio batteries.
- [ ] **Fridge prep:** put appliance thermometers in the fridge and freezer, make extra ice, and freeze water jugs and gel packs. (Details in [Water and food](#water-and-food-safety).)
- [ ] **Generator owners:** check fuel, oil, and extension cords. Decide now exactly where it will sit outdoors.
- [ ] **Check on people:** call older neighbors and relatives and ask what they need. Our [Winter Emergency Kit for Elderly Parents](/guides/winter-emergency-kit-for-elderly-parents/) has a full plan.

### When a Warning is issued (the last 12–24 hours)

- [ ] Drip faucets and open sink cabinets if a hard freeze is forecast.
- [ ] Bring pets inside and move outdoor animals to shelter with unfrozen water.
- [ ] Pick your **warm room** and set it up (see [During the outage](#during-the-outage-the-first-hours-and-days)).
- [ ] Fill a bathtub or a [waterBOB](/gear/waterbob/) for flushing and washing if you're on a well or a small water system that may lose pressure.
- [ ] Park cars where falling limbs are less likely to hit them, and away from power lines.
- [ ] Set your plan for checking in with family. The free [All Accounted](/apps/all-accounted/) app is a household check-in board that works offline and shares a text snapshot.
- [ ] Get off the roads before the ice starts.

For a tighter, hour-by-hour version of this countdown, use [How to Prepare for an Ice Storm in the South: 48-Hour Checklist](/guides/ice-storm-prep-checklist-south/), or the printable [Winter Storm Prep Planner](/shop/winter-storm-prep-planner/) in our shop if you want a seven-day paper version.

## Get the house ready

A house that holds heat buys you hours, sometimes days, when the furnace stops. Ready.gov recommends keeping out the cold with insulation, caulking, and weather stripping.

**Seal and insulate**

- [ ] Weather-strip and caulk drafty doors and windows.
- [ ] Add window insulation film to single-pane or leaky windows. A shrink-film kit like the [3M Indoor Window Insulator Kit](/gear/3m-indoor-window-insulator-kit/) is a cheap, renter-friendly option.
- [ ] Keep heavy curtains or spare blankets ready to hang over windows and doorways.
- [ ] Have draft stoppers or rolled towels for the gaps under doors.

**Outside**

- [ ] Look at the trees near your house, driveway, and service line. Dead or overhanging limbs are the ones ice brings down first. For trees touching power lines, call your utility; don't trim near lines yourself.
- [ ] Clean gutters so melting ice can drain.
- [ ] Disconnect and drain garden hoses.
- [ ] Have rock salt, a de-icer, or sand for steps and walkways. The [CDC notes](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) that many cold-weather injuries come from falls on icy sidewalks, steps, driveways, and porches.
- [ ] Store a snow shovel or ice scraper where you can reach it without going outside.

**Inside**

- [ ] Know where your electrical panel, water shut-off, and gas shut-off are, and keep a flashlight near each.
- [ ] Keep a headlamp per person, like the [Black Diamond Spot 400](/gear/black-diamond-spot-400/) or the [Wuben H1](/gear/wuben-h1/), plus a lantern for each main room, like the [Goal Zero Lighthouse 600](/gear/goal-zero-lighthouse-600/). The CDC recommends battery-powered flashlights and lanterns over candles, since candles can start house fires.
- [ ] Gather blankets, sleeping bags, hats, wool socks, and warm coats for everyone. The CDC lists extra blankets, sleeping bags, and warm coats as a backup heat source.

## Protect your pipes

Frozen and burst pipes are one of the most expensive parts of a Southern freeze, and they often show up after the storm, when the ice in the pipe thaws and the water starts pouring out.

The [CDC's winter storm guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) for very cold or freezing temperatures:

- [ ] Leave taps slightly open so they **drip continuously**.
- [ ] **Keep the temperature inside your home warm.**
- [ ] **Let heated air reach pipes**, for example by opening cabinet doors under kitchen and bathroom sinks. Move cleaners out of reach of kids and pets first.
- [ ] If pipes freeze, **don't thaw them with a torch**. Thaw them slowly with warm air from an electric hair dryer.
- [ ] If you can't thaw them or a pipe has broken, use bottled water or get water from a neighbor.

Southern homes need extra attention in a few places: crawl spaces, pipes on exterior walls, attic lines, garage lines, outdoor hose bibs, and the exposed plumbing under mobile and manufactured homes. If your local water utility asks people not to drip faucets to protect system pressure, follow the utility.

**If a pipe bursts:** shut off the main water valve right away, turn off electricity to any area where water is reaching outlets or appliances (only if you can reach the panel safely and stay dry), and call a licensed plumber.

The full walkthrough, including which pipes freeze first and how to tell if one is frozen, is in [How to Keep Pipes From Freezing in a Southern Cold Snap](/guides/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/).

## Heat safely: CO alarms, generators, and fuel heaters

This is the most important section on the page. When the power goes out in winter, people get cold and reach for whatever will make heat. Carbon monoxide (CO) is the danger. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) describes it as an odorless, colorless gas that can cause sudden illness and death, and it says people who are asleep can die from CO poisoning before they have symptoms.

### Carbon monoxide alarms

- [ ] Ready.gov says to install CO detectors **with battery backup in central locations on every level** of your home ([Ready.gov: Power Outages](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages)).
- [ ] The CDC says to put battery-operated or battery-backup CO detectors **near every sleeping area**.
- [ ] Replace detectors following the manufacturer's instructions **or every 5 years**, per the CDC, and check the batteries twice a year when the clocks change.
- [ ] Plug-in-only alarms stop working when the power goes out. Make sure you have at least one battery-powered alarm, like the [Kidde KN-COB-LP2](/gear/kidde-kn-cob-lp2/) or the [Eton Blackout Buddy CO detector](/gear/eton-blackout-buddy-co/), which also works as a flashlight.

**Know the symptoms.** The CDC lists headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion, often described as "flu-like." If the alarm sounds or anyone feels these symptoms, get everyone, pets included, outside into fresh air right away and call 911.

### Never use these for heat indoors

Per the CDC and Ready.gov:

- **Generators**, inside the home, basement, or garage, even with doors and windows open.
- **Gas or charcoal grills and camp stoves**, inside the home, basement, or garage, or near windows.
- **A gas oven or stovetop** to heat the house. Ready.gov notes it also raises the risk of fires and burns and can damage the appliance.
- **Charcoal**, ever, indoors.
- **Portable flameless chemical heaters**, which the CDC says not to use indoors.

### Generator rules

| Rule | Source |
|---|---|
| Use generators and fuel only outdoors, at least **20 feet** from windows, doors, and attached garages. | [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages) |
| Place it more than **20 feet** from any window, door, or vent, where rain and snow can't reach it. | [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) |
| Never run it in the home or garage, even with doors and windows open. | [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) |
| Keep it dry. Touching a wet generator or devices connected to one can cause electrical shock. | [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages) |
| Connect appliances with heavy-duty, outdoor-rated extension cords. | Ready.gov, CDC |
| Let it cool before refueling. Fuel spilled on hot engine parts can ignite. | [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather) |
| Don't store gasoline indoors where fumes could ignite. | [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) |
| Run a battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarm inside while the generator runs. | [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) |
| Follow the manufacturer's instructions. | Ready.gov |

**Never plug a generator into a wall outlet.** Feeding power back through your house wiring can hurt you and utility crews working on the lines. If you want a generator to power household circuits, have a licensed electrician install a transfer switch.

### Fuel-burning heaters, fireplaces, and wood stoves

The CDC's rules for wood stoves, fireplaces, and fuel heaters:

- Use fireplaces, wood stoves, and other combustion heaters only if they're **properly vented to the outside** and don't leak exhaust into the room.
- Make sure you have proper ventilation if you must use a kerosene heater, and check with your local fire department that kerosene heaters are legal where you live.
- Use only the fuel your heater is designed for. Don't substitute.
- Don't burn paper in a fireplace.

If you use an indoor-rated propane heater, such as the [Mr. Heater Portable Buddy](/gear/mr-heater-portable-buddy/), follow the manufacturer's ventilation instructions exactly and run a battery-powered CO alarm in the same room.

### Electric space heaters (when you have power or a big enough battery)

From the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html):

- Use heaters with **automatic shut-off** and non-glowing elements.
- Keep them **at least 3 feet** from drapes, furniture, bedding, and anything flammable. Never cover one.
- Never put one on furniture or near water, and never leave children alone near one.
- Avoid extension cords, and don't run the cord under rugs.
- Stop using any heater with a damaged cord or that sparks.

A space heater draws a lot of power, so it will drain a portable power station fast. ## Water and food safety

### Water

- [ ] Store at least **1 gallon per person per day, and per pet, for at least 3 days**. FoodSafety.gov suggests storing more for hot climates, pregnant women, and people who are sick ([FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency)).
- [ ] Replace stored water (other than store-bought) **every 6 months**, and watch expiration dates on bottled water, per FoodSafety.gov.
- [ ] Keep a bottle of **unscented** household bleach for disinfecting water and cleaning. FoodSafety.gov suggests replacing it each year because it loses strength.
- [ ] Sturdy containers like the [Reliance Aqua-Tainer](/gear/reliance-aqua-tainer/) are easier to store and pour than a pile of small bottles.
- [ ] If your water utility issues a **boil-water notice**, follow it. The CDC says bringing water to a rolling boil for one minute kills most germs.

How much is enough for your house? Run the [Water Storage Calculator](/tools/water-calculator/), and read our [emergency water storage guide](/guides/emergency-water-storage/) for containers and filters. More options are in [Water & Food Storage](/emergency-water/).

### Food: before the power goes out

From [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency):

- [ ] Put **appliance thermometers** in the fridge and freezer. The freezer should be at or below 0°F and the fridge at or below 40°F.
- [ ] Make ice and freeze gel packs and water containers ahead of time. Melting ice from frozen water jugs also gives you drinking water.
- [ ] Freeze leftovers, milk, and fresh meat you won't need right away.
- [ ] Group food together in the freezer so it stays cold longer.
- [ ] Have coolers ready in case the power may be out for more than 4 hours.

### Food: during and after an outage

| | Safe time with the door closed | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator | About **4 hours** if unopened | [FoodSafety.gov](https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/food-safety-in-disaster-or-emergency), [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages) |
| Full freezer | About **48 hours** | FoodSafety.gov, Ready.gov |
| Half-full freezer | About **24 hours** | FoodSafety.gov |

- Keep the doors closed as much as you can.
- **Don't put perishable food out in the snow.** FoodSafety.gov warns that outdoor temperatures vary and food can be exposed to animals and unsanitary conditions. Instead, fill buckets or jugs with water, set them outside to freeze, and use that ice in the fridge or coolers.
- **Throw out** perishable food (meat, poultry, fish, eggs, leftovers) that has been above 40°F for 2 hours or more. FoodSafety.gov also says to discard refrigerated perishables when the power has been off for 4 hours or more.
- **Freezer food** can be refrozen or cooked if it still has ice crystals or is 40°F or below.
- You can't judge safety by look or smell. When in doubt, throw it out.

Shelf-stable food and outdoor cooking gear are in [Water & Food Storage](/emergency-water/) and [Homestead & Multi-Purpose](/homestead-multi-purpose/). Remember that camp stoves and grills are outdoor-only.

## Medical and power-dependent needs

A power outage can make medical devices stop working, and Ready.gov lists that as one of the main dangers of an outage. Plan this part first if it applies to your household.

- [ ] **Talk to your doctor or medical provider** about a power outage plan for powered medical devices and refrigerated medicines, and ask how long each medication can be stored at higher temperatures ([Ready.gov: Power Outages](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages)).
- [ ] **Call your power company** and ask to be put on its list for priority power restoration if someone uses electric medical equipment at home ([Ready.gov: People with Disabilities](https://www.ready.gov/disability)). Ask your county emergency management office whether it keeps a voluntary registry for people who may need help.
- [ ] **Keep extra batteries charged** for power wheelchairs and other battery-powered medical or assistive devices.
- [ ] **Have a cooler and ice packs** ready for medicines that need refrigeration.
- [ ] Know that Ready.gov says if the power is out for more than a day, you should **discard medication that should be refrigerated unless the label says otherwise**, and contact your doctor or pharmacist for a new supply.
- [ ] If anyone is on **dialysis or other life-sustaining treatment**, know more than one facility that can help.
- [ ] Keep a written list of medications, doses, allergies, device model and serial numbers, and doctor contacts, plus extra glasses and hearing-aid batteries.
- [ ] Decide in advance **where you'll go** if the home can't stay warm or keep a device running: a relative with power, a hospital, or a warming center. Ready.gov says to go to a community location with power if heat or cold is extreme.

**Backup power for devices:** a portable power station can run some devices for a while, but check the device's power draw against the station's capacity and ask the device maker or your provider before you rely on it. The free [Generator & Power Station Sizer](/tools/power-sizer/) helps you estimate.

**Babies and older adults:** the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) says infants under one year should never sleep in a cold room, and if you can't keep the home warm, make arrangements to stay elsewhere. It also says older adults make less body heat, so check on older neighbors often.

## Pets and livestock

Ready.gov says to [include pets in your family's emergency plan](https://www.ready.gov/pets) and to bring them indoors at the first sign or warning of a storm.

- [ ] **Bring pets inside** before the storm. The NWS notes that animals feel wind chill too.
- [ ] Pack **several days of food** in an airtight, waterproof container, plus water and a bowl. Count pets in your water math (1 gallon per pet per day, per FoodSafety.gov).
- [ ] Keep an **extra supply of any medicine** your pet takes.
- [ ] Have a **collar with ID**, a backup leash, a carrier, and copies of registration and medical records. Ready.gov recommends microchipping and keeping the contact info current.
- [ ] Keep a **photo of you with your pet** in case you get separated.
- [ ] Know that **many public shelters and hotels don't allow pets inside**, so line up a pet-friendly place to go ahead of time.
- [ ] Set up a **buddy system** with a neighbor who can care for your pets if you can't get home.

**Horses, goats, and other large animals:** make sure every animal has ID, plan shelter and unfrozen water ahead of time, and if you might need to move them, do it early, as Ready.gov advises. The [Homestead Ice Storm Kit](/kits/homestead-ice-storm-kit/) covers rural water and power.

The free [Collar Card](/apps/collar-card/) app keeps a pet emergency profile and kit checklist with expiry dates, offline on your phone.

## Vehicles and travel

The best plan for an ice storm is to **not drive in it**. The [NWS](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-ice-frost) says bridges and overpasses freeze before other surfaces and warns about black ice, which is hard to see and most common in early morning hours after melted snow or rain refreezes.

**Before the storm:**

- [ ] Fill the gas tank. Ready.gov recommends keeping a full tank in winter.
- [ ] Ready.gov's car kit list includes **jumper cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, bottled water, and non-perishable snacks**. A lithium jump starter like the [NOCO Boost GB40](/gear/noco-boost-gb40/) means you don't need a second car.
- [ ] Add a phone charger, an ice scraper, and a bright cloth to signal for help.

**If you must drive:**

- Let someone know your route and when you expect to arrive, as the CDC advises.
- Never pour hot water on an iced windshield. The CDC says it can make the windshield shatter.
- Slow down, even if the road just looks wet, per the NWS.

**If you get stranded,** the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) and [NWS](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-during) say:

- [ ] Stay in the vehicle.
- [ ] Run the motor and heater for **about 10 minutes per hour**, with a window opened slightly.
- [ ] **Keep snow away from the exhaust pipe** to reduce the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning.
- [ ] Tie a brightly colored cloth to the antenna or door, and turn on the dome light at night while the engine runs.
- [ ] Wrap up in extra clothing and blankets, keep moving your arms and legs, and huddle together for warmth.
- [ ] Don't eat snow. It lowers your body temperature.

**At home:** the CDC says never to run a car or truck inside an attached garage, even with the garage door open. Don't sit in an idling car in the garage to warm up or charge a phone.

Full checklist: [What to Keep in Your Car for Winter](/guides/winter-car-emergency-kit/), the [Car Winter Kit](/kits/car-winter-kit/), and the [Vehicle Emergency Kits](/vehicle-emergency-kits/) lane.

## Protect your accounts and documents

If you have to leave home, or if a computer or a filing cabinet is damaged, you'll want copies of IDs, insurance policies, and other key papers you can still reach. [Ready.gov's emergency kit list](https://www.ready.gov/kit) includes copies of insurance policies, identification, and bank account records, saved electronically or in a waterproof, portable container. [Ready.gov's financial preparedness page](https://www.ready.gov/financial-preparedness) also suggests storing important documents in a safety deposit box, on an external drive, or in the cloud so you can get to them during a disaster.

A password manager can keep logins in one place, and many also let you store secure copies of documents — IDs, insurance cards, and policies — so you can open them from a phone if you have to leave the house. [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/use-strong-passwords) recommends a password manager for creating and storing long, unique passwords, and notes that some are free (including the one built into your browser) and some cost money. <a href="https://www.dpbolvw.net/click-101929817-13868703" target="_blank" rel="sponsored nofollow noopener" data-program="nordpass">NordPass</a> is one paid subscription option. Use a strong, unique passphrase for the manager itself, and turn on multi-factor authentication if it's offered.

Keep paper copies too, in a waterproof container you can grab quickly, in case phones and internet are down.

**On shelter or public Wi-Fi.** If you end up at a warming center, hotel, or other public network, treat that connection as untrusted. [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/good-security-habits) says public networks are not very secure, which makes it easier for others to intercept your data, and that if you connect to an open network you can use a virtual private network (VPN) to help keep your traffic private. <a href="https://www.anrdoezrs.net/click-101929817-12814518" target="_blank" rel="sponsored nofollow noopener" data-program="nordvpn">NordVPN</a> is a paid subscription VPN. A VPN is optional, not a requirement, and it doesn't replace careful habits: confirm the network name with staff when you can, avoid entering sensitive information if you have a better option (such as your phone's cellular data), and be cautious with unexpected login pages.

## During the outage: the first hours and days

### The first 30 minutes

- [ ] **Check whether it's just you.** Look at your breaker panel and whether neighbors' lights are out.
- [ ] **Report the outage** to your utility through its app, website, or phone line. Don't assume someone else did. Official outage maps for Mississippi utilities are on our [Mississippi local resources page](/local/mississippi/).
- [ ] **Unplug sensitive electronics.** Ready.gov notes power can return with surges that damage equipment. Leave one lamp switched on so you know when power is back.
- [ ] **Switch to flashlights and lanterns**, not candles.
- [ ] **Turn on the weather radio** and save your phone battery with low-power mode.
- [ ] **Note the time** the power went out. That starts your food-safety clock. The [Outage Timer](/tools/outage-timer/) can track it for you.

### Set up one warm room

From the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html) and [NWS](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-during):

- Close off rooms you don't need.
- Stuff towels or rags in the cracks under doors.
- Close curtains or cover windows with blankets, especially at night.
- Avoid opening outside doors more than you have to.
- Dress in loose layers. The CDC says wool, silk, or polypropylene hold more body heat than cotton. Wear a hat, and remove a layer if you start sweating, because damp clothes chill you.
- Eat regular meals and drink warm, sweet drinks or broth. The CDC advises avoiding alcohol and caffeine, which make your body lose heat faster.

A small interior room with few windows is usually easiest to keep warm. Pick a room with a battery CO alarm if you're using any fuel-burning heater.

### Power: what to run first

If you have a power station or a generator running safely outdoors, prioritize:

1. Medical devices and medication cooling
2. Phones and a weather radio
3. Lights
4. The refrigerator, in short stretches, if your setup can handle it
5. Internet router, so you can get updates

The [Runtime Reserve](/apps/runtime-reserve/) app logs generator fuel and runtime and estimates how many hours you have left, offline. The [Solar & Power Station Sizer](/tools/solar-sizer/) helps if you're planning to recharge with solar panels after the storm. Portable panels such as the [Anker SOLIX PS100](/gear/anker-solix-ps100/) or [PS400](/gear/anker-solix-ps400/) are one option; confirm connector and input compatibility with your power station, and remember that solar only helps when there is usable sun.

### Watch for cold-related illness

**Hypothermia** warning signs in adults, per the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html): shivering, exhaustion or feeling very tired, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, and drowsiness. In babies: bright red, cold skin and very low energy. The CDC and [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather) both say a body temperature **below 95°F is an emergency**. Get medical help right away. If you can't, Ready.gov says to move the person to a warm room, warm the center of the body first (chest, neck, head, and groin), and keep them dry and wrapped in blankets.

**Frostbite** signs: numbness, white or grayish-yellow skin, or skin that feels unusually firm or waxy, usually on the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers, and toes. Ready.gov says to go to a warm room and soak in warm water, and **not** to massage the area or use a heating pad. Seek medical care.

### When to leave

Leave for a relative's house, a hotel, or a warming center if the house can't be kept warm, if someone has a medical device you can't power, if there's an infant who would otherwise sleep in a cold room, or if a CO alarm goes off. Ready.gov says to check with local officials about warming locations; our [local resources pages](/local/) link to official sources for warming centers by county.

The step-by-step version of this section is [What to Do When the Power Goes Out in a Mississippi Ice Storm](/guides/power-outage-ice-storm-mississippi/), and the shorter checklist is [Winter Power Outage Checklist](/guides/winter-power-outage-checklist/). For staying safe around ice, falling limbs, and downed lines, see the [Ice Storm Survival Checklist for the South](/guides/ice-storm-survival-checklist-for-the-south/).

## After the storm

**Outside**

- [ ] **Stay away from downed power lines** and anything touching them, including fences, puddles, and tree limbs. Assume every line is live and report it to your utility.
- [ ] **Watch for falling limbs.** Ice-loaded branches can keep dropping after the storm ends.
- [ ] **Walk carefully.** Salt or sand steps and walkways, and wear traction cleats like [Yaktrax Pro](/gear/yaktrax-pro/) if you have to go out.
- [ ] **Don't overdo the cleanup.** The CDC says cold weather puts extra strain on the heart, and people with heart disease or high blood pressure should follow their doctor's advice about shoveling or heavy work. Work slowly and take breaks.
- [ ] If you use a chainsaw, follow the manufacturer's safety instructions, and leave limbs tangled in power lines to the utility.

**Inside**

- [ ] **Check the food** using the FoodSafety.gov rules above. When in doubt, throw it out.
- [ ] **Check medicines** that needed refrigeration, and call your pharmacist if the power was out for more than a day.
- [ ] **Check for frozen or burst pipes** before you turn water fully back on, and watch for leaks as pipes thaw.
- [ ] **Plug electronics back in slowly** once power is steady.
- [ ] **Make sure the furnace and water heater restart normally.** The NWS says to make sure a gas furnace vent isn't blocked by a snowdrift once it's safe to go out, so check that outdoor vents aren't covered by ice or snow.
- [ ] **Take photos** of any damage for insurance before you clean up, and keep receipts for repairs. If you made the document copies in [Protect your accounts and documents](#protect-your-accounts-and-documents), you'll have the policy information on hand.
- [ ] **Restock** what you used so you're ready next time.

## Southern special cases

### Mobile and manufactured homes

- Plumbing under the home is often more exposed than in a site-built house. Check the skirting for gaps, insulate any exposed supply lines, and follow the manufacturer's instructions for any heat tape or heat cable.
- If you heat with propane, make sure the tank is filled before the storm and that the area around the tank and regulator is clear.
- Use only heaters approved for your home, keep them at least 3 feet from anything that can burn (per the CDC), and have a battery CO alarm and smoke alarm.
- Mobile homes can lose heat quickly. Have a plan to go somewhere warmer if the outage looks long.

### Electric heat and heat pumps

Many Southern homes heat with electricity only, so an outage means no heat at all. During extreme cold, utilities sometimes ask customers to conserve power, and grid operators may order rotating outages. Plan as if your heat could go out even if your lines don't come down.

### Rural water systems and wells

- If you're on a well, the pump needs electricity. Fill containers and a bathtub before the storm for drinking, washing, and flushing.
- Small water systems can lose pressure or issue boil-water notices after a freeze. Have stored water and follow your utility's instructions.

### Rare storms, long distances

Because a big ice storm may come only every few years, stores sell out fast once one is forecast. Building your kit in the fall, before the rush, is the easiest win on this list. Rural households should plan on being on their own for longer, since stores, pharmacies, and help are farther away on icy roads.

## Common questions

**Where can I see if a warning is in effect right now?**
Start with the [What's happening now](/#live-status) board on the home page, or open [live alerts](/alerts/). Both use official National Weather Service data. You can also sign up for occasional official-source notes on [Local & community safety updates](/updates/).

**I live on the Mississippi Gulf Coast. Do these steps still apply?**
Yes. Ice, freeze, and long outages happen on the coast too, even if they are less frequent than in the hills. Use the same heat, pipe, food, and carbon monoxide steps, and keep your county emergency-management and outage-map links handy: [Harrison](/local/mississippi/harrison/), [Hancock](/local/mississippi/hancock/), and [Jackson County](/local/mississippi/jackson/).

**November is also tornado season here. Should I watch for that too?**
Yes. NWS Jackson notes that November marks the peak of Mississippi's second severe-weather season. If thunderstorms are in the forecast alongside a cold front, read [Tornado Watch vs. Warning in Mississippi and the Gulf South](/guides/tornado-watch-vs-warning-mississippi/) and keep the same alert tools handy.
