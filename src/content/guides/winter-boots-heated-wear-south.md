Winter in the South rarely means a foot of powder. It means freezing rain, a glaze of ice on the steps, slush in the driveway, and sometimes several days without power. Most of us don't own real winter gear because we only need it a few days a year. But those few days are when wet feet, a slip on the porch or a cold house without heat can turn into a real problem.

This guide covers what to wear for a Southern winter storm: boots, socks, traction, and battery-heated layers, plus the layering basics the CDC recommends.

## Why Southern winters are different

The National Weather Service warns that [even small accumulations of ice can be extremely dangerous](https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-ice-frost) to drivers and people on foot, and that ice storms can leave damage utility crews need time to repair. That is why Southern ice storms bring so many outages and falls.

What that means for clothing:

- **Waterproof matters more than extreme cold ratings.** Wet feet get cold fast, even at temperatures just below freezing.
- **Traction matters as much as warmth.** The CDC notes that [many cold-weather injuries come from falls on ice](https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html)-covered sidewalks, steps, driveways and porches.
- **You may be cold indoors too.** In an outage, warm clothes you can wear inside the house are as useful as anything you'd wear outside.

## Start with the CDC's layering basics

The CDC's winter storm guidance gives a simple formula. Adults and children going outside should wear a hat, a scarf or knit mask, sleeves snug at the wrist, mittens (the CDC notes they are warmer than gloves), a water-resistant coat and boots, and several layers of loose-fitting clothing.

The CDC describes three layers:

1. **Inner layer:** fabrics that hold body heat and don't absorb moisture, like wool, silk or polypropylene. These hold more heat than cotton.
2. **Insulation layer:** something that traps warm air, like wool, down or fleece.
3. **Outer layer:** tightly woven, and ideally water- and wind-resistant.

Two more CDC tips that matter in a Southern storm: **stay dry**, because wet clothing chills the body quickly, and **take off a layer if you start sweating**, because sweat makes you lose heat.

## Boots: waterproof first

For ice and slush, look for a boot that is waterproof up the shaft, insulated, and has a sole with a grippy tread. Rubber-and-neoprene boots are a popular fit for Southern winters because they handle slush, mud and cold rain, and you can pull them on fast to check the yard.

- **Leave a little wiggle room.** Tight boots squeeze your toes and cut circulation, which makes feet colder.

## Socks: the cheapest upgrade

Cotton socks soak up moisture and stay wet. Wool and synthetic blends handle moisture better, which lines up with the CDC's inner-layer advice. A cushioned wool sock like the [Darn Tough Hiker Boot Full Cushion](/gear/darn-tough-hiker-boot-full-cushion/) works in boots and around a cold house during an outage. Keep a dry spare pair by the door. Change socks if your feet get wet.

## Traction for icy steps and driveways

Even good boot soles slide on glare ice. Pull-on traction devices stretch over your boots and add grip for walking on ice. The [Yaktrax Pro](/gear/yaktrax-pro/) is one widely sold option. Take them off before walking on smooth indoor floors, where they can be slippery.

And follow the CDC's advice to avoid walking on ice when you can. Spread sand, rock salt or another de-icer on steps and walkways before you need them.

## Battery-heated vests and jackets

Heated clothing uses a rechargeable battery pack to warm panels in the garment. For Southern winters, it fills a useful gap. You get serious warmth on the few days you need it without buying a heavy parka you'll rarely wear. And it works indoors during an outage, where a heated vest under a sweater can keep your core warm in a cold house.

Some examples from TideWe:

- **A heated vest as a mid layer:** the [TideWe ThermShift](/gear/tidewe-thermshift-heated-vest/), sold for men and women as a low-profile vest, and the [TideWe ThermCore](/gear/tidewe-thermcore-heated-vest/), a double-sided fleece vest with adjustable heat, per TideWe.
- **A full heated jacket:** the [TideWe Inferno](/gear/tidewe-inferno-heated-jacket/), a waterproof heated jacket with a camo shell, built with hunting in mind.

Tips for heated wear:

- **A vest is the most flexible choice.** It layers under any coat and works indoors.
- **Battery life depends on the heat setting.** Makers list runtimes by setting. Use the lowest setting that keeps you comfortable.
- **Charge before the storm.** Keep the battery topped up when ice is in the forecast. A power bank or an Energy Reserve can recharge it during an outage.
- **Follow the maker's care instructions.** Remove the battery before washing, use the charger that came with it, and don't sleep in heated clothing unless the maker says that's safe.
- **Watch for skin irritation or hot spots,** especially for children, older adults, and anyone with reduced feeling in their skin.

## Know the warning signs

The CDC lists shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness as warning signs of hypothermia in adults. Signs of frostbite include white or grayish-yellow skin, skin that feels unusually firm or waxy, and numbness. **Constant shivering means it's time to go inside.** If a person's temperature is below 95°F, the CDC says it's an emergency: get medical help right away.

Check on older neighbors and relatives. The CDC notes older adults often make less body heat.

## A simple Southern winter clothing kit

For each person in your home:

- [ ] Waterproof, insulated boots that fit with winter socks
- [ ] Two or more pairs of wool or synthetic socks
- [ ] A base layer that isn't cotton
- [ ] A fleece or wool mid layer, or a heated vest
- [ ] A water-resistant outer jacket
- [ ] A hat, mittens and a scarf or knit mask
- [ ] Traction devices for icy steps (adults)

## The bottom line

For a Southern winter, think dry and steady first: waterproof boots, good socks and traction. Then add warmth in layers, with a heated vest as a flexible option that also helps inside a cold house during an outage.
